THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0015, Petition of Scott Marshall, the court
on December 2, 2025, issued the following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record, has
considered the oral arguments of the parties, and has determined to resolve the
case by way of this order. See Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The petitioner, Scott
Marshall, challenges a decision of the respondent, the Board of Trustees (the
Board) of the New Hampshire Retirement System (NHRS) concluding that he is
not entitled to certain retirement benefits because he was not in vested status
before January 1, 2012. See RSA 100-A:5, II (Supp. 2024). We conclude that
the petitioner waived his pre-2012 vested status. Accordingly, we affirm.
I. Background
The record supports the following facts. The petitioner began his
employment with the New Hampshire Department of Corrections (DOC) as a
corrections officer in 1994. He became a group II member of the NHRS and
paid into the NHRS while he was employed there. See RSA 100-A:1, X(b)
(Supp. 2024) (“‘Group II members’ shall mean permanent policemen and
permanent firemen.”); RSA 100-A:1, VII(d) (Supp. 2024) (providing that a “state
corrections officer” meets the definition of “‘[p]ermanent policeman’”). In 1998,
the petitioner ended his employment with the DOC and withdrew his
accumulated contributions from the NHRS. In 1999, the petitioner was rehired
by the DOC as a corrections officer, and he resumed paying into the NHRS.
On October 30, 2009, the petitioner was laid off from his position at the
DOC. At that time, the petitioner had over ten years of creditable service in the
NHRS and was in vested status. See Am. Fed’n of Teachers — N.H. v. State of
N.H., 167 N.H. 294, 303-04 (2015) (explaining that “‘vesting’ refers to the
period of time that a member must work to become eligible for a pension,” and
that “a member [of the NHRS] who completes ten years of creditable service
achieves ‘vested status’”). In December 2009, the petitioner completed an
application requesting a refund of his accumulated contributions to the NHRS
(refund application). See RSA 100-A:10, II(a) (2001) (providing that “[a] group II
member who has completed 10 years of creditable service and who, for reasons
other than retirement or death, ceases to be a permanent policeman” may, “[i]n
lieu of a vested deferred retirement allowance, . . . make application . . . and
receive a return of the member’s accumulated contributions”). The refund
application provided that, in consideration of the return of his contributions,
the petitioner waived all rights in any NHRS funds, and by his actions fully
terminated his membership in the NHRS and waived his vested status. In
January 2010, the petitioner’s accumulated contributions were refunded.
The petitioner was recalled from the layoff and his employment as a
corrections officer at the DOC was reinstated in February 2010. He rejoined
and resumed paying into the NHRS at that time. In 2020, the petitioner began
repaying his prior withdrawals from the NHRS. See RSA 100-A:3, VI(a)-(b)
(Supp. 2024) (providing that members who previously left the NHRS and
withdrew their contributions, and who later rejoin the NHRS, may repay their
withdrawals with interest to receive prior service credit). By 2023, the
petitioner had repaid all his previous withdrawals from the NHRS with interest
and his prior service credit had been reinstated.
In 2023, the NHRS issued an administrative determination that the
petitioner is a “Tier B member for NHRS purposes, (i.e., a member who had not
attained vested status prior to January 1, 2012 as provided in RSA 100-A:5,
II(a) . . . .).” The petitioner appealed the decision to the Board. He argued that
the NHRS erred because he was in vested status before January 1, 2012. He
also argued that, although he withdrew his NHRS contributions, his pre-2012
vested status was reinstated when he repaid those withdrawals with interest in
2020 through 2023. See RSA 100-A:3, VI (Supp. 2024).
After a hearing, the hearing officer recommended that the Board affirm
the NHRS’s administrative determination because the petitioner’s “purchase of
prior service credits after January 1, 2012, did not retroactively make him ‘in
vested status’ on January 1, 2012.” The hearing officer explained that, after
the petitioner reentered the NHRS in 2010, the legislature amended the statute
in 2011 to divide group II members into three “Tiers.” (Quotation omitted.)
Tier A are members who were “in vested status” before January 1,
2012; Tier B are members who had commenced service prior to
July 1, 2011, but had not attained vested status prior to January
1, 2012; and Tier C are individuals who commenced service on or
after July 1, 2011, and who had not attained vested status prior to
January 1, 2012.
(Footnote omitted.) RSA 100-A:5, II(b) and (d) (Supp. 2024) provide the highest
retirement annuity for tier A members, the second highest for tier B members,
and the lowest for tier C members.
The hearing officer concluded that, because the petitioner had most
recently rejoined the NHRS prior to July 1, 2011, but was not in vested status
prior to January 1, 2012, he is a tier B member for NHRS purposes subject to
the provisions of RSA 100-A:5, II(d). The hearing officer also concluded that,
because the petitioner had not repaid his withdrawals and restored his prior
service credit prior to January 1, 2012, he had fewer than four years of
2
creditable service on January 1, 2012 for the purposes of calculating his
retirement benefits. See RSA 100-A:5, II(d). The Board voted to accept the
hearing officer’s recommendation. The petitioner unsuccessfully moved for
reconsideration. This petition for a writ of certiorari followed.
II. Analysis
The petitioner argues that the Board erred in its interpretation of RSA
chapter 100-A. Because RSA chapter 100-A does not provide for judicial
review, a petition for a writ of certiorari is the sole remedy available to a party
aggrieved by a decision of the Board. See Petition of Malisos, 166 N.H. 726,
728 (2014). Certiorari is an extraordinary remedy that is not granted as a
matter of right, but rather at the court’s discretion. Petition of Dean, 177 N.H.
___, ___ (2025), 2025 N.H. 44, ¶9. Our review of an administrative agency’s
decision on a petition for a writ of certiorari entails examining whether the
agency has acted illegally with respect to jurisdiction, authority or observance
of the law or has unsustainably exercised its discretion or acted arbitrarily,
unreasonably or capriciously. Id. We exercise our power to grant such writs
sparingly and only where to do otherwise would result in substantial injustice.
Id.
The petitioner argues that the Board erred when it concluded that he was
not in vested status prior to January 1, 2012, and he asserts that he is
therefore entitled to group II, tier A membership in the NHRS pursuant to RSA
100-A:5, II(a). The respondent counters that the Board did not err because the
petitioner lost his pre-2012 vested status when he withdrew his accumulated
contributions, and asserts that the petitioner is therefore a group II, tier B
member subject to the provisions of RSA 100-A:5, II(d).
This question requires that we engage in statutory interpretation. We
review an agency’s statutory interpretation de novo. See Appeal of Tower Hill
Tavern, LLC, 177 N.H. ___, ___ (2025), 2025 N.H. 41, ¶8. We first look to the
language of the statute itself, and, if possible, construe that language according
to its plain and ordinary meaning. Id. We give effect to every word of a statute
whenever possible and will not consider what the legislature might have said or
add language that the legislature did not see fit to include. Id. We also
construe all parts of a statute together to effectuate its overall purpose. Id.
However, we do not construe statutes in isolation; instead, we attempt to
construe them in harmony with the overall statutory scheme. Id.
