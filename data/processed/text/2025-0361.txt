THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0361, State of New Hampshire v. Randy S.
Littlefield, the court on September 1, 2026, issued the following
order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The defendant, Randy S. Littlefield, appeals his conviction in
the Circuit Court (Cross, J.), of violating RSA 265:32 (2024) by failing to yield
the right of way. He argues that: (1) he was convicted under the wrong statute;
(2) his due process rights were violated; and (3) the evidence was insufficient to
support a finding of guilt. We affirm.
The defendant first argues that he was erroneously charged and found
guilty under RSA 265:31 (2024) (duties at stop signs and yield signs), instead
of RSA 265:32 (2024) (failure to yield from private road or driveway). However,
the complaint provided in the State’s appendix and the transcript from the trial
show that the defendant was, in fact, charged and found guilty of violating RSA
265:32 for failing to yield the right of way from a private driveway. Accordingly,
we find no error.
The defendant next argues that the State’s disclosure of a dashboard
camera video recording during trial violated his right to due process. To
preserve an evidentiary objection, a party must “make [the] objection to
evidence at the time it is offered, or at the earliest opportunity after the reason
for objection becomes apparent.” State v. Bell, 175 N.H. 382, 396 (2022)
(quotation omitted). The defendant did not object to the State’s playing of the
dashboard camera video recording at trial. Accordingly, we conclude that the
defendant did not preserve this issue for appeal. See Broderick v. Watts, 136
N.H. 153, 169 (1992) (objection to improper cross-examination questions not
preserved when raised for first time in post-trial motion); see also In the Matter
of Birmingham & Birmingham, 154 N.H. 51, 56 (2006) (self-represented
litigants are bound by the same procedural rules that govern parties
represented by counsel).
To the extent that the defendant argues that the prosecution failed to
disclose the existence of a recording from the cruiser’s rear-facing camera, he
has failed to demonstrate that he raised this issue in the trial court. See Bean
v. Red Oak Prop. Mgmt., 151 N.H. 248, 250 (2004). Accordingly, we conclude
that the issue is not preserved for review.
The defendant next argues that the evidence was insufficient to prove
that he violated RSA 265:32. When considering a challenge to the sufficiency
of the evidence, we review the record to determine whether any rational trier of
fact could have found guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, considering the
evidence, and all reasonable inferences drawn therefrom, in the light most
favorable to the State. State v. Seibel, 174 N.H. 440, 445 (2021). The
defendant bears the burden of proving that the evidence was insufficient to
prove guilt. Id.
RSA 265:32 provides that “[t]he driver of a vehicle about to enter or cross
a way from a private road or driveway shall yield the right of way to all vehicles
approaching on said way.” The officer testified that she observed the
defendant’s vehicle “entering traffic without a reasonable enough space to do
so.” The defendant argues that the trial court improperly credited the officer’s
testimony because, he asserts, it was contradicted by the dash camera footage.
“[E]valuation of witness credibility, resolving conflicts in the testimony, and
determining the weight to be given to the evidence are matters within the
province of the [trier of fact.].” State v. Alwardt, 164 N.H. 52, 57 (2012). The
trial court, after viewing the dashboard camera video recording, concluded that
the defendant failed to yield to the vehicles in the roadway, finding that he
“clearly cut off those cars” and “failed to yield to that other traffic.” We
conclude that a rational fact-finder could have found guilt beyond a reasonable
doubt. See Seibel, 174 N.H. at 445.
The defendant argues that as he began to enter the roadway, his vision
was impaired by the glare of the sun. He argues that the trial court erred by
excluding his exhibit showing the location of the sun at the time of the
violation. First, the record fails to show that the trial court excluded his
exhibit. In addition, it was for the trial court to evaluate the credibility of the
defendant’s testimony and determine the weight of the evidence. See Alwardt,
164 N.H. at 57. We conclude that a rational fact-finder could have found
beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant violated RSA 265:32. See id.
To the extent that the defendant’s brief may be construed to raise
additional issues, we conclude that they were not preserved, see Bean v. Red
Oak Prop. Mgmt., 151 N.H. 248, 250 (2004); are not sufficiently developed, see
2
State v. Blackmer, 149 N.H. 47, 48 (2003), and otherwise do not warrant
further discussion, see Vogel v. Vogel, 137 N.H. 321, 322 (1993).
Affirmed.
Donovan, Countway, Gould, and Will, JJ., concurred.
