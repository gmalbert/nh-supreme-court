COUNTWAY, J.
[¶1] The defendant, Dominic Cherbonneau, appeals his convictions
following a jury trial in Superior Court (Anderson, J.) on one count of
attempted first degree assault with a firearm, see RSA 631:1, I(b) (2016); RSA
629:1 (2016), and two counts of riot, see RSA 644:1, I(a), IV (2016). On appeal,
the defendant argues that the trial court erred when it denied his motion for
judgment notwithstanding the verdict (JNOV) on the basis that there was
insufficient evidence to support his convictions, including insufficient evidence
that the State disproved that his conduct was justified as self-defense. He also
argues that the trial court erred when it sentenced him on both riot charges
where the charges were based on the same conduct. We agree with the
defendant that the evidence was insufficient because it failed to prove beyond a
reasonable doubt that he did not act in self-defense and we therefore reverse.
Accordingly, we need not address whether the trial court erred when it
sentenced the defendant on both riot counts.
[¶2] The jury could have found the following facts, based in part on
surveillance videos which were played for the jury throughout the trial and
which captured some of the events described below. The defendant was with a
group of people at a restaurant in Manchester one night in November 2022.
Members of the defendant’s group were drinking and playing pool. At some
point, C.M., one of the alleged victims, entered the restaurant and proceeded to
the bar area, across the room from where the defendant’s group was playing
pool. Several minutes later, a member of the defendant’s group (Subject #1)
left the pool table area and pushed through the crowd toward another part of
the restaurant. The defendant and other members of his group followed
Subject #1. According to one witness, a “skirmish” then unfolded between
members of the defendant’s group and C.M., which involved “pushing, shoving,
[a] little commotion.”
[¶3] Following the “skirmish,” C.M. exited the restaurant through the rear
emergency exit door. The defendant’s group returned to the pool table.
Subject #1 proceeded to exit the restaurant through the front door, followed
shortly thereafter by the defendant and other members of their group. The
other alleged victim, C.G., also exited through the front door shortly after the
defendant’s group.
[¶4] Approximately one minute later, Subject #1 and others in the
defendant’s group followed C.M. into a pharmacy parking lot across the street
from the restaurant. C.G. was walking with the group following C.M. C.G.
then approached C.M. and the two stood near each other. Subject #1, C.G.,
C.M. and others engaged in a verbal altercation. Around the same time, the
defendant and his friend, who had been with the defendant’s group inside the
restaurant, arrived and stood near the entrance of the parking lot, away from
the altercation. As the argument between Subject #1, C.G., C.M. and others
continued, the group congregated near a silver Hyundai, and the defendant
moved behind a nearby vehicle parked across from the Hyundai.
[¶5] Approximately thirty seconds after the defendant moved behind the
nearby vehicle, he stepped out from behind the vehicle and moved closer to the
2
altercation, a few feet away from the Hyundai. There was then an exchange of
gunfire between C.M. or C.G. at the rear of the Hyundai and others standing
near the front of the Hyundai. The defendant did not fire a weapon during this
exchange. In response to this gunfire, several people ran out of the parking lot.
[¶6] After the exchange of gunfire, the defendant retreated back behind
the nearby vehicle. C.M. and C.G., who had been standing toward the rear of
the Hyundai, moved to the driver’s side of the Hyundai. Seconds later, as
either C.M. or C.G. emerged from behind the Hyundai, the defendant rose from
his position on the ground, moved forward and fired his handgun in the
direction of C.M. and C.G. C.M. or C.G. dropped to the ground. A bullet from
the defendant’s gun struck the window of another restaurant across the street.
[¶7] The defendant ran out of the parking lot and back toward the
restaurant. A witness testified that, after hearing gunshots, he observed the
defendant put a gun “in his waistbelt” before getting into a vehicle and leaving
the scene. Police arrived at the scene and found C.G. suffering from a gunshot
wound to her arm. C.M. placed a pistol on the ground and informed a
responding officer that it had been fired. Police discovered eight spent
cartridge casings and two live rounds in the area of the nearby vehicle where
the defendant was standing when he fired his handgun.
[¶8] The defendant was charged with eight counts of attempted first
degree assault and two counts of riot. One riot count alleged that the
defendant participated in a riot while armed with a firearm, and the other riot
count alleged that as a result of the riotous conduct, C.G. suffered a bullet
wound to her arm. Neither count alleged that the defendant fired the shot that
injured C.G.’s arm.
[¶9] After the State rested its case at trial, the defendant moved to
dismiss all charges on the ground that the State had failed to disprove that he
acted in self-defense. The trial court denied the defendant’s motion but
concluded that there was sufficient evidence to warrant a self-defense
instruction, and accordingly instructed the jury that the State had to prove
beyond a reasonable doubt “that the [d]efendant’s conduct was not justified
based on this defense.” The jury found the defendant guilty on one count of
attempted first degree assault with a firearm and both counts of riot. The jury
acquitted the defendant on the other seven counts of attempted first degree
assault. The defendant filed a motion for JNOV, arguing in part that there was
insufficient evidence for the jury to conclude that he did not act in self-defense.
The trial court denied the motion. This appeal followed.
[¶10] The defendant first argues that the trial court erred in denying his
motion for JNOV because the State failed to disprove that he acted in self-
defense with respect to both the attempted first degree assault with a firearm
charge and the riot charges. See RSA 627:1 (2016); State v. McMinn, 141 N.H.
3
636, 644-45, 647 (1997) (holding that defendant was entitled to self-defense
instruction on riot charges). A motion for JNOV presents a question of law,
which we review de novo. State v. Carnevale, 172 N.H. 700, 703 (2019). In
reviewing a ruling for JNOV, we will uphold the jury’s verdict unless no rational
trier of fact could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, considering all the
evidence and all reasonable inferences therefrom in the light most favorable to
the State. Id.; see also State v. Pennock, 168 N.H. 294, 307 (2015). The trial
court cannot weigh the evidence and if the evidence at trial is conflicting, or if
several reasonable inferences may be drawn, the motion for JNOV should be
denied. See Carnevale, 172 N.H. at 703-04. On appeal, the defendant bears
the burden of demonstrating that the evidence was insufficient to disprove that
he acted in self-defense. See Pennock, 168 N.H. at 307.
[¶11] Because self-defense is a pure defense under New Hampshire law,
State v. Munroe, 173 N.H. 469, 474 (2020); see RSA 627:1, when evidence of
self-defense is admitted, conduct negating the defense becomes an element of
the charged offense that the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt, see
Munroe, 173 N.H. at 474; see RSA 626:7, I(a) (2016). RSA 627:4, II(a) provides
that “[a] person is justified in using deadly force upon another person when he
reasonably believes that such other person . . . [i]s about to use unlawful,
deadly force against the actor or a third person.” RSA 627:4, II(a) (2016). Even
if a person is justified in using deadly force under RSA 627:4, II(a), however,
that justification is nullified under certain circumstances. See RSA 627:4, II,
III (2016). As relevant here, RSA 627:4, III(a) provides that a person is not
justified in using deadly force to defend against deadly force by another “if he
or she knows that he or she . . . can, with complete safety . . . [r]etreat from the
encounter.” RSA 627:4, III(a). The statute further provides, however, that a
person “is not required to retreat if he or she is . . . anywhere he or she has a
right to be, and was not the initial aggressor.” Id. Thus, in this case, in order
for the State to carry its burden at trial of disproving that the defendant acted
in self-defense, it had to prove beyond a reasonable doubt either that the
defendant’s conduct was not justified under RSA 627:4, II(a), or that the
defendant knew that he could, “with complete safety . . . [r]etreat from the
encounter,” and that he had a duty to do so, RSA 627:4, III(a).
[¶12] In denying the defendant’s motion for JNOV, the trial court did not
address whether the State’s evidence disproved that the defendant had a
reasonable belief that another person was about to use unlawful, deadly force
against him or another person. We construe the court’s order as assuming
without deciding that the State failed to disprove that the defendant’s conduct
was justified under RSA 627:4, II(a). See State v. Surrell, 171 N.H. 82, 88
(2018) (“The interpretation of a trial court order presents a question of law for
us to decide.”). Instead, the court focused on the duty to retreat set forth in
RSA 627:4, III(a), concluding that the jury could have found that the defendant
had a duty to retreat because he “was not in a place that he had a right to be
as there was evidence that he was in the . . . parking lot after the [pharmacy]
4
had closed.” The court also reasoned that, alternatively, the jury could have
found that the defendant had a duty to retreat because the “[d]efendant and
his group were the initial aggressors, that [C.M.] fired his weapon in self-
defense, and that [the] [d]efendant failed to withdraw from the encounter.” The
court further concluded that, in addition to finding that the defendant had a
duty to retreat, the jury could have also found that the defendant “had the
ability to retreat with complete safety from the encounter with [C.M.].” The
defendant argues that these conclusions were erroneous.
[¶13] As an initial matter, the defendant also contends that the State’s
evidence on the self-defense element had to exclude all rational conclusions
other than self-defense because that evidence was circumstantial. See State v.
Vincelette, 172 N.H. 350, 354 (2019) (providing that “[w]hen the evidence as to
an element of proof is solely circumstantial, it must exclude all reasonable
conclusions except guilt”). We disagree. The State’s evidence included a
surveillance video from the pharmacy parking lot. We observe that the video
recording is of limited quality and, based on the location and angle of the
surveillance camera and the fact that the video captures events occurring at
night and contains no audio, it does not clearly illustrate each of the events
that transpired in the parking lot, including C.M.’s and C.G.’s actions in the
moments before the defendant fired his weapon. But the video nonetheless
generally depicts the defendant’s conduct and surroundings, the actions and
locations of other people, the duration of the incident, and other circumstances
the jury could consider in assessing both the reasonableness of the defendant’s
belief that the use of deadly force was necessary, see RSA 627:4, II(a), and
whether he had a duty to retreat and could do so with complete safety, see RSA
627:4, III(a). This video thus constituted direct evidence of whether the
defendant acted in self-defense, and we therefore disagree with the defendant
that our circumstantial evidence standard applies in this case. See State v.
Blevins, 10 N.W.3d 29, 39-40 (Minn. 2024) (holding in self-defense case that
surveillance videos of incident “provide[d] direct evidence of what they show,
including [the defendant’s] surroundings and the actions of the [other
participants],” and noting that other courts have reached similar conclusions).
[¶14] Nonetheless, based on our review of the record, we agree with the
defendant that the trial court erred in concluding that there was sufficient
evidence that the defendant had a duty to retreat. First, no rational trier of
fact, viewing all the evidence and reasonable inferences therefrom in the light
most favorable to the State, could have found that the State had proven beyond
a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not have a right to be in the parking
lot. See RSA 627:4, III(a) (providing that defendant has duty to retreat unless
defendant is “anywhere he or she has a right to be, and was not the initial
aggressor”); RSA 626:7, I(a). There was no testimony that the pharmacy was
closed or that the defendant and others were otherwise prohibited from being
in the parking lot. We thus agree with the defendant that the jury “had no
5
evidentiary basis from which to conclude that [he] was not in a place where he
was entitled to stand his ground to defend himself.”
[¶15] The record also reveals that no rational trier of fact, viewing all the
evidence and inferences drawn therefrom in the light most favorable to the
State, could have found beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant himself
was the initial aggressor in the encounter with C.M. There is no evidence from
which the jury could infer that the defendant instigated the “skirmish” with
C.M. inside the restaurant. With respect to the events in the parking lot, there
is also no evidence from which the jury could infer that the defendant led his
group outside or that he re-initiated the confrontation with C.M. The
defendant was not the first of his group to leave the restaurant — Subject #1
was. Although the defendant was with the group that followed C.M. from the
restaurant, he remained near the threshold of the parking lot for approximately
thirty seconds while Subject #1 and others approached C.M. The defendant
then walked into the parking lot toward the group but stepped behind a nearby
vehicle, which separated him from C.M., C.G., and the others.
[¶16] The jury could infer, as the State suggests, that the defendant’s
decision to stay removed from the rest of his group and take a position behind
the nearby vehicle was strategic in order to provide “flanking” support during
his group’s confrontation with C.M. This inference does not, however, support
a finding that the defendant himself was the initial aggressor. Based on the
evidence, the only reasonable inference that can be drawn is that the defendant
was not the first to confront C.M. or C.G. in the parking lot, and that he did not
fire any of the initial shots between the individuals at the front and those at the
back of the Hyundai. Even when all the evidence is viewed in the light most
favorable to the State, the evidence was insufficient for the jury to find that the
defendant started the encounter with C.M. Cf. State v. Newell, 141 N.H. 199,
205 (1996) (concluding that, under circumstances of this case, there was “no
meaningful distinction” between terms “initial aggressor” and “the one who
started the encounter” as used in self-defense jury instructions (quotation
omitted) (emphasis added)).
[¶17] The trial court thus erred to the extent it relied on the conduct of
the defendant’s group in denying the defendant’s motion for JNOV. The plain
language of RSA 627:4, III(a) focuses on whether the defendant was the initial
aggressor, not the defendant’s associates or companions. See RSA 627:4, III(a).
We find support for this interpretation in State v. Mayo, 167 N.H. 443 (2015).
There, we stated that RSA 627:4, III(a)’s limitation on the defensive use of force
“appl[ies] only to the person who actually takes the putatively defensive action,”
and held that the trial court erroneously instructed the jury that “if it found
that the defendant or the third person . . . was in fact the initial aggressor or
provoker, then the defendant’s actions in defense of [the third person] were not
justified.” Mayo, 167 N.H. at 451-52. Here, the evidence clearly established
that other members of the defendant’s group — primarily Subject #1 —
6
initiated the confrontation with C.M. both inside the restaurant and in the
parking lot. While the evidence supported a finding that members of the
defendant’s group were the initial aggressors, it does not support a finding that
the defendant himself was the initial aggressor.
[¶18] In sum, the only conclusions a rational trier of fact could reach
based on the evidence, even viewing the facts and inferences in the light most
favorable to the State, are that the defendant had a right to be in the parking
lot and that he was not the initial aggressor. Therefore, we conclude there was
insufficient evidence that the defendant had a duty to retreat from the
encounter, see RSA 627:4, III(a), and the trial court erred in denying his motion
for JNOV on this basis.
[¶19] This does not, however, end our inquiry. Because we disagree with
the trial court’s ruling that the jury could have concluded that the defendant
had a duty to retreat, we must next consider whether there existed an
alternative basis for the jury to conclude that the State met its burden of
disproving self-defense. Conduct is justified under RSA 627:4, II(a) if a
defendant had a reasonable belief that the other person was about to use
unlawful deadly force against him or another person, and that the amount of
force he used was necessary under the circumstances. See RSA 627:4, II(a);
State v. Rice, 169 N.H. 783, 786 (2017). Thus, to disprove the defendant’s
claim that he acted in self-defense, the State had to prove beyond a reasonable
doubt that the defendant either did not reasonably believe that unlawful deadly
force was about to be used against him or another person, or that the amount
of force he used exceeded what he reasonably believed to be necessary under
the circumstances. See RSA 627:4, II(a); Rice, 169 N.H. at 786. We therefore
must consider whether a rational trier of fact, viewing all the evidence in the
light most favorable to the State, could conclude that the evidence disproved
any one of these elements to establish self-defense under RSA 627:4, II(a). It is
the defendant’s burden on appeal to demonstrate that the evidence as to all of
these elements was insufficient. See Pennock, 168 N.H. at 307.
[¶20] As to the reasonableness of the defendant’s belief concerning the
imminence of the threat, the defendant argues that the evidence demonstrates
that he “had a reasonable belief that whoever had fired a shot from [behind the
Hyundai] seconds earlier was going to shoot again.” Based on our review of the
record, we agree with the defendant and conclude that no rational trier of fact,
viewing the evidence and drawing all inferences therefrom in the light most
favorable to the State, could find that the defendant lacked a reasonable belief
that another person was about to use deadly force against him or a third
person.
[¶21] The jury in this case, as in other self-defense cases, see State v.
Furgal, 164 N.H. 430, 436 (2012), was properly instructed to “consider how the
[d]efendant acted under the circumstances as they were presented to him at
7
the time and not necessarily as they appear on detach[ed] reflect[ion].” The
evidence shows that the defendant observed or heard an exchange of gunfire
occur near the Hyundai, which caused other members of his group to scatter.
Although the gunfire had ceased, C.M. or C.G. was still armed and it was not
clear that hostilities had ended. Approximately twelve seconds after the initial
gunfire ended, the defendant observed C.M. or C.G. emerge from a place of
concealment in a dark parking lot. Based on this evidence, we cannot
conclude that the evidence supported a finding that the defendant lacked a
reasonable belief that the use of deadly force by C.M. or C.G. against him or
another person was imminent. See RSA 627:4, II(a).
[¶22] Next, we further conclude that the State failed to prove beyond a
reasonable doubt that the defendant lacked a reasonable belief that C.M. or
C.G. was about to use unlawful force against him or another person. See RSA
627:4, II(a); cf. Mayo, 167 N.H. at 453-54. On this issue, the trial court noted
that the evidence supported a finding that C.M. acted in self-defense, which, if
true, would mean that C.M.’s resort to deadly force was lawful under the
circumstances. See RSA 627:4, II(a). The evidence did not establish, however,
that the defendant was or should have been aware of who initiated the
exchange of gunfire across the Hyundai, or that the defendant otherwise
should have recognized that either C.M.’s or C.G.’s conduct amounted to lawful
self-defense. See id. Consequently, even if we agree that the evidence
objectively established that either C.M. or C.G. fired a weapon in lawful self-
defense against other persons, we cannot conclude that a rational trier of fact
could have found that the defendant either knew or should have known as
much in the seconds following the gunfire between the other individuals. See
Furgal, 164 N.H. at 436; cf. Mayo, 167 N.H. at 454.
[¶23] Lastly, based on the evidence, no rational trier of fact could have
concluded that the amount of force used by the defendant exceeded what he
reasonably believed was necessary under the circumstances. See Rice, 169
N.H. at 786. It is undisputed that the defendant did not fire the first shots.
The jury thus could not rationally conclude that the defendant’s subsequent
use of his firearm was not “proportioned to the apparent danger” of the
situation. State v. Etienne, 163 N.H. 57, 75 (2011) (quotation omitted).
[¶24] In conclusion, we agree with the defendant that the evidence failed
to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he either had a duty to retreat from
the encounter or lacked a reasonable belief that his use of deadly force was
justified under the circumstances.1 See RSA 627:4, II(a), III(a). We therefore
1 Although the dissent concludes that a rational trier of fact could have found, beyond a
reasonable doubt, that the defendant could not have reasonably believed that the use of deadly
force by C.M. or C.G. was imminent, we note that, based on the limited quality of the parking lot
video and for the reasons already discussed, we disagree that a rational trier of fact could have
reached this conclusion.
8
agree with the defendant that the trial court erred in denying his motion for
JNOV, and we accordingly reverse his convictions. We therefore need not
address whether the trial court erred when it sentenced the defendant on both
riot charges.
Reversed.

WILL, J., with whom FRIZZELL, J., superior court justice, specially
assigned under RSA 490:3, II, joins, dissenting.
[¶25] I respectfully dissent because I conclude that the majority does not
faithfully adhere to our governing standards of review and, as a result,
undertakes an analysis and makes conclusions that our decisional law
ordinarily would, and should, leave to the jury.
[¶26] When faced with a motion for JNOV on evidentiary sufficiency, we
require the trial court to uphold the jury’s verdict unless no rational trier of
fact could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, considering all the evidence
and all reasonable inferences therefrom in the light most favorable to the State.
See State v. Spinale, 156 N.H. 456, 463 (2007). We emphasize that the trial
court “cannot weigh the evidence or inquire into the credibility of the
witnesses.” Id. (quotation omitted). We also emphasize that “if the evidence
adduced at trial is conflicting, or if several reasonable inferences may be
drawn, the motion should be denied.” Id. (quotation omitted) (emphasis
added). On appeal, we promise to confine ourselves to “objectively review[ing]
the record to determine whether any rational trier of fact could have found the
essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 464
(quotation omitted). Those standards should lead us to affirm the trial court.
[¶27] To set the table, I agree with the majority that the surveillance
video capturing the events in the pharmacy parking lot constitutes direct
evidence of whether the defendant acted in self-defense, meaning that our rule
about purely circumstantial evidence does not bear on the analysis. See State
v. Vincelette, 172 N.H. 350, 354 (2019). And, just months ago, we considered
and rejected an argument that we should carve out direct video evidence from
other forms of evidence for de novo review on appeal. See State v. Wells, 177
N.H. 769, 772-74 (2026), 2026 N.H. 8, ¶¶4-7. We confirmed that video
evidence “is no different than other evidence,” and that we must review it
“under the familiar standard that calls on us to view all of the evidence and all
reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the State and then
9
determine whether a rational jury could have found guilt beyond a reasonable
doubt.” Id. at 772, 2026 N.H. 8, ¶4 (emphasis added). We came to our
conclusion in Wells on our acknowledgment that the jury views video evidence
in the context of all of the other evidence, including live testimony from other
witnesses describing what the video shows and what it does not show, and
narrating the events the video captured. See id. at 773, 2026 N.H. 8, ¶6. As
with other evidence, therefore, “[t]he jury, as the finder of fact, [is] in a better
position than this court to view the video evidence in the context of the
witnesses’ testimony.” Id.
[¶28] If we hew to those standards, all of which tilt to the finder of fact on
factual findings and to the jury’s verdict, a rational trier of fact plainly could
have found, beyond a reasonable doubt, that RSA 627:4, II(a) does not provide
a justification for the defendant’s conduct. See Spinale, 156 N.H. at 463-64;
RSA 627:4, II(a) (2016) (providing that “[a] person is justified in using deadly
force upon another person when he reasonably believes that such other person
. . . [i]s about to use unlawful, deadly force against the actor or a third
person”). More specifically, a rational jury reviewing the evidence could have
found, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant could not have
reasonably believed that either C.M. or C.G. was about to use unlawful deadly
force against him or a third person. See State v. Rice, 169 N.H. 783, 786
(2017) (“A person is justified in using deadly force against another person when
he reasonably believes that the other person is about to use unlawful deadly
force against himself or a third person and he reasonably believes that the
amount of force he uses is necessary under the circumstances.”); State v.
Perez, 177 N.H. 211, 218 (2025), 2025 N.H. 6, ¶19 (“A belief that is
unreasonable, even though honest, will not support the defense.”). As a result,
the trial court did not err in denying the defendant’s motion for JNOV on self-
defense.2
[¶29] The video evidence rightly serves as the majority’s focal point, since
it comprises the primary evidence relevant to the defendant’s self-defense
claim. And I agree that a rational trier of fact could have interpreted the video
evidence as the majority does and could have drawn the majority’s conclusions
from it. But the video footage is amenable to a different set of observations and
conclusions more favorable to the State, and it is in that light that we require
the evidence to be taken.
[¶30] To begin, a rational trier of fact could have found that the
defendant could not have reasonably believed that C.M. or C.G. would use

[¶36] I do not offer this counter-interpretation of the evidence as my own
nor do I offer it by way of arguing that a rational jury could not have found the
version of the facts set forth by the majority; a jury certainly could have found
the facts as the majority suggests. Rather, I believe that because a rational
jury could have aligned with my alternative narrative of the evidence beyond a
reasonable doubt, the very standards we have devised for trial courts faced
with JNOV motions require that we affirm the trial court’s denial of the JNOV
regarding self-defense. See id.
[¶37] It bears brief reiteration that our jurisprudence talismanically
recites a JNOV standard of review that favors the jury’s verdict: the trial court
“uphold[s] the jury’s verdict unless no rational trier of fact could find guilt
beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 463 (quotation omitted) (emphasis added).
Logically consistent with our JNOV standard, we emphasize that if any conflict
exists in the trial evidence, or if the evidence allows for more than one
reasonable inference, we require trial courts to deny JNOV motions. See id.
Equally germane to my conclusion, we have very recently emphasized that
when reviewing a trial record on appeal, including any video evidence, we must
consider all the evidence and all reasonable inferences that flow from it in the
light most favorable to the State. See Wells, 177 N.H. at 772, 2026 N.H. 8, ¶4.
As I demonstrate, the evidence can be viewed in a light more favorable to the
State than the majority credits it. At a minimum, however, the counter-
narrative reveals conflicts in the evidence and the reasonable inferences that
flow from it that preclude a JNOV.
[¶38] I acknowledge that I have laid out a painstaking, moment-by-
moment narrative of the evidence. But I believe it necessary, as it is the kind of
close, painstaking analysis that we must assume the jury undertook in the
deliberation room, and because it is how the trial court, and this court, must
consider the evidence in an appeal of the denial of a motion for JNOV. Our
JNOV standards require, in essence, that we scour the evidence for
interpretations that support the verdict. See Spinale, 156 N.H. at 463-64. This
is the essence of taking the evidence, and drawing all reasonable inferences, in
the light most favorable to the State. I respectfully conclude that the majority,
by a less rigorous analysis, inadvertently usurps the jury’s role.
