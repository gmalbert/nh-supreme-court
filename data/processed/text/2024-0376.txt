THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2024-0376, In the Matter of Zachary Zieroff and
April Zieroff, the court on July 21, 2025, issued the following
order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The petitioner, Zachary Zieroff, appeals a final decree
entered by the Circuit Court (Carroll, R., approved by Boyle, J.) in his divorce
from the respondent, April Zieroff, following a final evidentiary hearing at which
he failed to appear. The petitioner argues that the trial court erred by dividing
the marital property in an unequal manner, and by determining his income for
purposes of awarding the respondent alimony. We affirm.
The trial court has broad discretion in fashioning a final divorce decree.
In the Matter of Spenard & Spenard, 167 N.H. 1, 3 (2014). Its discretion
includes decisions concerning alimony and property distribution. Id. We will
not overturn the trial court’s rulings on such matters absent an unsustainable
exercise of discretion, reviewing the record only to determine whether it
contains an objective basis to sustain the court’s discretionary judgments. Id.
If the trial court could reasonably have reached its findings on the evidence
before it, they will stand. Id. We assume that the trial court made all findings
of fact necessary to support its general rulings. See In the Matter of Dube &
Dube, 163 N.H. 575, 579 (2012). We defer to the trial court’s judgment in
evaluating the credibility of the witnesses and determining the weight of the
evidence presented. In the Matter of Aube & Aube, 158 N.H. 458, 465 (2009).
RSA 458:16-a, II (2018) requires the trial court to divide marital property,
including marital debt, see Maldini v. Maldini, 168 N.H. 191, 195 (2015)
(stating that “marital property” includes “marital debt” for purposes of property
division), in a manner that is equitable, see In the Matter of Sarvela & Sarvela,
154 N.H. 426, 431 (2006). When dividing marital property, the court must
presume that an equal division is equitable unless it determines, after
considering one or more statutory factors, that an equal division would not be
equitable or appropriate. RSA 458:16-a, II; Sarvela, 154 N.H. at 430-31. Such
factors include the “duration of the marriage,” the “age, health, social or
economic status, occupation, vocational skills, employability, separate
property, amount and sources of income, needs and liabilities of each party,”
and the “opportunity of each party for future acquisition of capital assets and
income.” RSA 458:16-a, II(a), (b), & (c). The court is not required to divide the
property by some mechanical formula, but in a manner that it deems just
based on the evidence presented and the equities of the case. Sarvela, 154
N.H. at 431. The trial court “shall specify written reasons for the division of
property which it orders.” RSA 458:16-a, IV (2018).
When parties to a divorce contest the amount of income generated by a
business owned by one of the parties for purposes of awarding alimony, it is
generally the burden of the party owning the business to produce information,
such as evidence of the business’s reasonable and necessary expenses, that is
necessary to accurately determine the business’s income. See In the Matter of
Hampers & Hampers, 166 N.H. 422, 440-41 (2014). It is likewise the burden of
the appealing party, in this case the petitioner, to provide this court with those
portions of the trial court record, including any relevant pleadings or
evidentiary documents, that are necessary to decide the issues raised. See
Bean v. Red Oak Prop. Mgmt., 151 N.H. 248, 250 (2004); In the Matter of
Rokowski & Rokowski, 168 N.H. 57, 62 (2015) (applying Bean to appealing
party’s failure to provide exhibits admitted into evidence at evidentiary
hearing); Estate of Day v. Hanover Ins. Co., 162 N.H. 415, 422 (2011) (applying
Bean to failure to provide relevant pleadings in the trial court). We assume
that relevant portions of the record not provided on appeal support the result
reached by the trial court. See Bean, 151 N.H. at 250.
The record establishes that in January 2024, shortly before the then-
scheduled final hearing, the trial court granted the petitioner’s expedited
motion to continue the hearing on the basis that his counsel had withdrawn
and that his current counsel needed additional time to prepare. The court
scheduled a seven-hour final hearing for May 7, 2024. On April 18, 2024, at a
hearing in a related proceeding between the parties, the petitioner’s current
counsel confirmed that, although he had not yet filed an appearance, he would
be representing the petitioner at the May 7 final hearing.
On May 6, the day before the final hearing, the petitioner’s counsel filed
both an appearance and an emergency motion to continue. The respondent
objected, and the Trial Court (Curran, J.) denied the emergency motion on the
same day. At the May 7 final hearing, neither the petitioner nor his counsel
appeared. Accordingly, the trial court defaulted the petitioner and proceeded
with the final hearing on the respondent’s offer of proof. We note that the
petitioner neither challenges the trial court’s denial of the emergency motion to
continue nor argues that the hearing notice failed to notify him that the court
might hold an immediate hearing on the contested issues if he failed to appear.
See Douglas v. Douglas, 143 N.H. 419, 426-27 (1999) (noting that trial court
may immediately hold a hearing on division of marital property upon a party’s
failure to attend a final divorce hearing if the hearing notice clearly stated that
the court could do so).

In her offer of proof, the respondent represented that the parties had
been married since June 10, 2014, that they purchased the marital home in
2017 for approximately $269,000 to $270,000, and that a recent certified
market analysis showed the home’s value to be between $564,980 and
$675,000. Although the respondent noted that the petitioner “was trying to
assert that [she was] wrong about [the home’s value] and that it was worth
approximately $440,000,” the trial court observed in its final order that the
petitioner had provided no basis for his estimate. The respondent asserted
that, although the petitioner had been ordered to pay the mortgage on the
home, he had paid only half the mortgage for April 2024 and had not yet paid
the mortgage for May, which had been due on May 1.
The respondent further asserted that at the April 18 hearing in the
related matter, the petitioner’s counsel had represented that in addition to
monthly “VA benefits” of $4,265 that the petitioner had disclosed in his July
2023 interrogatory responses, he now owned a business with his brother for
which he was working “70-plus” hours per week, and that the new business
was “bringing in very substantial income.” The petitioner had not, however,
supplemented his interrogatory responses to disclose the new income. See
Fam. Div. R. 1.25(E)(9). Nor had the petitioner provided an updated financial
affidavit disclosing the new business and any income that it generated. See
Fam. Div. R. 2.16(A). The respondent emphasized that she had asked specific
questions in her June 2023 interrogatories relative to any self-employment
income the petitioner received because he had claimed to be unemployed, and
because he owned another business. The respondent additionally noted that
she had recently been able to gain access to certain of the petitioner’s bank
accounts, and that the accounts revealed “[s]ome strange transfers and
surprising numbers” in addition to the VA benefit that were not previously
disclosed, including recent deposits of $2,000 on April 22 and $1,800 on May
2. By contrast, the respondent filed a financial affidavit with the court at the
final hearing, showing her monthly income to be $4,326 based upon her
employment as an assistant in an oil distribution business.
Finally, the respondent represented that she had opened a credit card
account during the marriage “only because [the petitioner had] persuaded her”
to open it so that he could fund the rebuilding of an engine on his motorcycle.
The respondent noted that there was a balance on the card likely in excess of
$7,457. Although the petitioner had been ordered to make monthly payments
on the card, he had not been making the required payments, and there was an
outstanding motion for contempt regarding the matter that the trial court
acknowledged having reviewed.
