THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2026-0251, T.N. v. J.M., the court on August
31, 2026, issued the following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The plaintiff, T.N., appeals the order of the Circuit Court
(Murray, R., approved by Burns, J.), following a hearing, denying her request
for a domestic violence final order of protection from the defendant, J.M. See
RSA 173-B:5 (2022). We construe her brief to argue that: (1) the trial court’s
decision is contrary to the weight of the evidence; (2) the court erred in its
evidentiary rulings and management of the proceedings; and (3) the trial judge
should have recused himself. We affirm.
To be granted a domestic violence final order of protection, the plaintiff
must establish, by a preponderance of evidence, that the defendant engaged in
“abuse.” RSA 173-B:5, I. “Abuse” means the commission or attempted
commission of one or more enumerated crimes by a family or household
member or a current or former sexual or intimate partner when such conduct
“constitute[s] a credible present threat” to the plaintiff’s safety. RSA 173-B:1, I
(2022); see S.C. v. G.C., 175 N.H. 158, 163 (2022).
So long as there is some evidentiary support for them, the trial court’s
“findings of facts shall be final, but questions of law may be transferred from
the circuit court to the supreme court.” RSA 173-B:3, VI (2022). We review
sufficiency of the evidence claims as a matter of law, upholding the trial court’s
findings and rulings unless they lack evidentiary support or are tainted by legal
error. S.C., 175 N.H. at 162. We defer to the trial court’s judgments as to the
credibility of the witnesses and the weight of their testimony. Id. at 162-63.
The plaintiff testified that she sought a protective order “because of the
violence and emotional mental stress that [the defendant has] put me through.”
However, in her brief testimony, the plaintiff did not describe any specific
conduct by the defendant that would constitute “abuse” as defined in RSA 173-
B:1, I. The trial court found that the plaintiff failed to meet her burden of
proof. Based upon the evidence presented, we find no legal error in the trial
court’s conclusion that the plaintiff failed to meet her burden to prove that the
defendant “abused” her.
The plaintiff argues that the evidence she presented in a prior hearing
that morning in a related case “would also be submitted in my [case] and was
not.” The record does not support the plaintiff’s argument. The parties agreed
that the hearings would remain separate. After the hearing in the related case,
the trial court gave the plaintiff “the opportunity to present [her] case.” The
court reminded the plaintiff that “[t]hey are separate cases, and that she would
“have to decide what [she] want[ed] to introduce into evidence.”
At the plaintiff’s request, and without objection from the defendant, the
court admitted each of the exhibits that the plaintiff introduced in the prior
hearing, including the text message from the plaintiff’s medical provider that
allegedly supported the plaintiff’s request for a protective order. Based upon
this record, we conclude that the plaintiff has failed to show that the trial court
unsustainably exercised its discretion in its management of the proceedings or
evidentiary rulings. See In the Matter of Conner & Conner, 156 N.H. 250, 252
(2007); Figlioli v. R.J. Moreau Cos., 151 N.H. 618, 626 (2005); (we review trial
court’s decisions on the management of proceedings and admissibility of
evidence for unsustainable exercise of discretion).
Finally, the plaintiff argues that the trial judge erred by not recusing
himself. The plaintiff did not raise this issue prior to filing her motion to
reconsider, in which she alleged that the defendant worked with the judge
“between 2015-2017.” A party seeking a judge’s disqualification must raise the
ground for disqualification as early in the case as possible. Appeal of Local
Gov’t Ctr., 165 N.H. 790, 812 (2014). In her objection, the defendant described
this allegation as “a complete fabrication.” Nothing in the record supports the
plaintiff’s position that the trial judge was required to recuse himself. We do
not consider the document in the plaintiff’s appendix allegedly supporting her
position because it was not presented to the trial court. See Flaherty v. Dixey,
158 N.H. 385, 387 (2009) (on appeal, we consider only evidence and documents
presented to trial court). However, if we were to consider the document, we
would reach the same conclusion.
To the extent that the plaintiff’s brief may be construed to raise
additional issues, we conclude that they were not preserved, see Bean v. Red
Oak Prop. Mgmt., 151 N.H. 248, 250 (2004); are not sufficiently developed, see
State v. Blackmer, 149 N.H. 47, 48 (2003), or otherwise do not warrant further
discussion, see Vogel v. Vogel, 137 N.H. 321, 322 (1993).
The defendant’s request for attorney’s fees is denied. See Sup. Ct. R. 23.
Affirmed.
Donovan, Countway, Gould, and Will, JJ., concurred.
