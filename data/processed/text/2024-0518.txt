On that issue, the parties agree that the applicable threshold standard is
the essential need test as stated in State v. Zarella, 177 N.H. 328, 341-42
(2025), 2025 N.H. 20, ¶36. Because the parties agree on the applicable
standard, we assume without deciding that the essential need test applies here
— where the State is requesting that a State entity disclose records in its
possession to a State-designated evaluator. Cf. Zarella, 177 N.H. at 334-38,
2025 N.H. 20, ¶¶10-25 (distinguishing standard applicable in cases involving
defendant’s request for privileged records in the government’s possession from
standard applicable when defendant seeks such records held by a private
entity); In re Kathleen M., 126 N.H. 379, 380-81, 385 (1985) (applying essential
need test to determine whether State could introduce at involuntary admission
hearings privileged testimony from respondents’ treating psychiatrists at New
Hampshire Hospital; decided under prior version of statutory privilege).
We held in Zarella that, to obtain in camera review and subsequent
disclosure of privileged records, the party seeking disclosure must establish an
essential need for the records sought. See Zarella, 177 N.H. at 331, 341, 2025
N.H. 20, ¶¶1-2, 36. To show essential need, a party must “prove both that
there is a compelling justification for the disclosure of the records, and that the
targeted information is unavailable from another source.” Id. at 341, 2025
N.H. 20, ¶36.
We turn first to the compelling justification prong of the essential need
test because it is dispositive. The trial court reasoned that “[a]bsent a review of
Defendant’s medical records, the evaluation preceding the State’s attempt to
involuntarily admit [the defendant] will not be as accurate.” It determined that
this “inaccuracy potentially jeopardizes the safety of Defendant and the public,”
giving rise to a compelling justification for piercing the statutory privileges. The
court’s reasoning was informed by the evaluator’s testimony at the hearing
that, generally, a review of “reasonably available records” is the “standard of
care” when performing a forensic psychiatric assessment and that, although it
is possible to do an evaluation without reviewing the records, “[t]he accuracy of
that evaluation would be less than if we had access to those records.”
We agree with the defendant that, if accepted, the trial court’s reasoning
would amount to a per se rule that there is a compelling justification to pierce
a defendant’s statutory privileges whenever the State seeks the defendant’s
records to facilitate an evaluation for the appropriateness of involuntary
admission pursuant to RSA 135:17-a, V. We have previously declined to adopt
such per se rules regarding compelling justifications for piercing statutory
privileges. See Stewart, 178 N.H. at ___, 2026 N.H. 14, ¶21 (observing that “the
prosecution of felonies is not, standing alone, a per se compelling justification,”
