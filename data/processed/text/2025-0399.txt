THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0399, State of New Hampshire v. Marc
Vilgrain, the court on September 10, 2026, issued the following
order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted on
appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See Sup. Ct.
R. 20(3). The defendant, Marc Vilgrain, appeals his convictions, following a
bench trial in Circuit Court (Ashley, J.), on two misdemeanor counts of cruelty to
animals under RSA 644:8, III(h) (Supp. 2025) and RSA 644:8-aa (2016). On
appeal, the defendant argues that the trial court erred by denying his motion to
dismiss the case on the ground that the State had failed to introduce sufficient
evidence to prove his guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. We reverse.
“A challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence raises a question of law,
which we review de novo.” State v. Seibel, 174 N.H. 440, 445 (2021). “When
considering such challenges, we objectively review the entire record to determine
whether any rational trier of fact could have found guilt beyond a reasonable
doubt, considering the evidence, and all reasonable inferences drawn therefrom,
in the light most favorable to the State.” Id. “We examine each item of evidence
in the context of the entire case, and not in isolation.” Id. “The trier of fact may
draw reasonable inferences from facts proved as well as from facts found as the
result of other inferences, provided they can be reasonably drawn therefrom.” Id.
“The defendant bears the burden of proving that the evidence was insufficient to
prove guilt.” Id.
“If the evidence presented at trial consists of both direct and circumstantial
evidence, we apply the standard set forth above and uphold the verdict unless no
rational trier of fact could have found guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.” Seibel,
174 N.H. at 445. However, if the defendant challenges the sufficiency of the
evidence supporting a finding for which the trial record contains only
circumstantial evidence, “the defendant must establish that the evidence fails to
exclude all reasonable conclusions except guilt.” Id. “The proper analysis is not
whether the evidence excludes every possible conclusion consistent with
innocence, but whether it has excluded all reasonable conclusions other than
guilt.” Id. “We do not determine whether the defendant has suggested another
possible hypothesis that could explain the events in an exculpatory fashion.” Id.
“Rather, we evaluate the evidence in the light most favorable to the State and
determine whether the alternative hypothesis is sufficiently reasonable that a
rational trier of fact could not have found proof of guilt beyond a reasonable
doubt.” Id. “Where solely circumstantial evidence is at issue, the critical
question is whether, even assuming all credibility resolutions in favor of the
State, the inferential chain of circumstances is of sufficient strength that guilt is
the sole rational conclusion.” Id. (quotation and brackets omitted).
In this case, the defendant was charged with cruelty to animals for
“negligently permit[ting] an animal in his custody, a black pitbull, to be subjected
to unnecessary suffering when [the defendant] did leave the dog in a motor
vehicle without relief leading to the animal’s death,” see RSA 644:8, III(h), and for
“negligently confin[ing] a dog in a motor vehicle in which the temperature was so
high that it caused serious harm — death — to occur,” see RSA 644:8-aa. At the
close of the State’s case, the defendant moved to dismiss the charges on the
ground that the State had failed to introduce sufficient evidence to prove guilt
beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court denied the defendant’s motion from
the bench, and found him guilty on both counts. This appeal followed.
On appeal, the defendant argues that the trial court erred by denying his
motion to dismiss for lack of sufficient evidence. Specifically, the defendant
contends that the State failed to prove: (1) that the temperature in the car was
the cause of the dog’s death, as required by RSA 644:8-aa; and (2) that the dog
had been subjected to unnecessary suffering, as required by RSA 644:8, III(h).
We agree.
The State failed to adduce any direct evidence as to the cause of the dog’s
death or whether it had suffered. Although the police officers involved in the
investigation and the animal control unit agreed that a necropsy ought to be
performed by a veterinarian in order to determine the cause of the dog’s death,
no such examination was ever performed. Further, although the record reflects
that the high temperature that day was 79 degrees, the evidence was mixed as to
whether the vehicle’s windows were down, or whether only one had been left
“slightly cracked” open. Moreover, the State failed to adduce any direct evidence
establishing, for example, the actual temperature inside the vehicle or the
temperature of the dog’s body, or even any circumstantial evidence, for example,
whether the vehicle was parked in the sun or in the shade, whether there was
any wind or humidity that day, or whether the dog had exhibited any signs of
distress. Nor did the State adduce evidence as to the age or overall health of the
dog, or as to the normal temperature range that such a dog could comfortably
tolerate. Although a lay witness testified that the dog appeared healthy based on
her brief observation in passing a few days earlier, and although the investigating
police officer testified that the dog did not appear to be injured, there was no
evidence that either witness had any relevant expertise or familiarity with the
dog’s actual condition. Additionally, although the State adduced evidence that
there were “little dimples” on the inside of the vehicle’s rear doors that were
consistent with scratch marks that a dog might make, there was no dispute that
the defendant owned another dog and no evidence that the “dimples” were recent
or a sign of distress.
2
In sum, based upon our review of the record, viewing the evidence and all
reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the State, we conclude that
the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the temperature in the
car was the cause of the dog’s death or that the dog had been subjected to
unnecessary suffering. Because the State’s solely circumstantial evidence failed
to exclude any number of other reasonable conclusions, such as that the dog
died from an underlying health condition, old age, or some other occurrence, the
inferential chain of circumstances here is not of sufficient strength that guilt is
the sole rational conclusion. See Seibel, 174 N.H. at 445. Accordingly, the
defendant’s convictions for cruelty to animals under both RSA 644:8, III(h) and
RSA 644:8-aa are reversed.
Reversed.
Donovan, Countway, Gould, and Will, JJ., concurred.
