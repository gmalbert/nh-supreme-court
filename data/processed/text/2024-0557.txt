THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2024-0557, Laurie A. Ortolano v. City of
Nashua, the court on August 21, 2025, issued the following
order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The plaintiff, Laurie A. Ortolano, appeals the order of the
Superior Court (Temple, J.), denying her motions for contempt and for a
protective order against the defendant, the City of Nashua. The plaintiff argues
that the defendant unnecessarily delayed in producing documents responsive
to her Right-to-Know Law request, see RSA 91-A:4, I (2023), and that the court
erred in denying her motions as moot. We affirm.
On August 18, 2023, this court affirmed the trial court’s order requiring
the defendant to conduct a reasonable search of its back-up tapes for records
responsive to the plaintiff’s Right-to-Know Law request. See Ortolano v. City of
Nashua, 176 N.H. 175 (2023). On October 11, 2023, the day after the court’s
mandate, the defendant started reviewing the back-up tapes, applying
necessary redactions pursuant to RSA 91-A:5 (2023), and producing the
requested documents in batches. On October 17, 2023, the trial court closed
the case.
On February 21, 2024, the plaintiff moved to reopen the case. She also
moved for a finding of contempt and for a protective order. The plaintiff
asserted that the defendant’s method of responding to her Right-to-Know Law
request was designed to frustrate and delay the production of records. She
sought the court’s assistance in obtaining the documents more expeditiously.
On June 28, 2024, the trial court held an evidentiary hearing on the
plaintiff’s motions. There is no dispute that, on May 28, 2024, one month prior
to the hearing, the defendant produced the last remaining batch of documents
responsive to the Right-to-Know Law request. Following the hearing, the court
issued an order finding that the issues raised in the plaintiff’s motions were
moot.
The plaintiff argues that the trial court erred in denying her motions as
moot. “The doctrine of mootness is designed to avoid deciding issues that have
become academic or dead.” Sullivan v. Town of Hampton Bd. of Selectmen,
153 N.H. 690, 692 (2006) (quotation omitted). “[T]he question of mootness is
not subject to rigid rules, but is regarded as one of convenience and
discretion.” Id. (quotation omitted). “Usually, unless a pressing public interest
is involved, or the question is capable of repetition yet evading review, an issue
that has already been resolved is not entitled to judicial intervention.” Appeal
of Hinsdale Fed. of Teachers, 133 N.H. 272, 276 (1990) (quotation and citation
omitted).
In this case, the trial court found that the issues raised in the plaintiff’s
motions no longer required judicial intervention. Implicitly, the trial court also
found that none of the exceptions to the mootness doctrine applied. Based
upon this record, we cannot conclude that the trial court erred in denying the
plaintiff’s motions on mootness grounds.
In light of our decision, we need not address the plaintiff’s remaining
arguments. See Antosz v. Allain, 163 N.H. 298, 302 (2012) (declining to
address parties’ other arguments where holding on one issue is dispositive).
Affirmed.
MacDonald, C.J., and Donovan and Countway, JJ., concurred.
