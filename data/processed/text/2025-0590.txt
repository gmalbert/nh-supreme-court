THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0590, O.U. v. M.W., the court on June 3,
2026, issued the following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The defendant, M.W., appeals a domestic violence final order
of protection, see RSA 173-B:5, I (2022), issued by the Circuit Court
(Panebianco, J.) in favor of the plaintiff, O.U., the defendant’s former wife. The
defendant argues that the trial court erred by finding that he engaged in
“abuse,” see RSA 173-B:1, I (2022), and by denying, without a hearing, his
motion to vacate the protective order based upon the plaintiff’s alleged perjury.
We affirm.
We first address the defendant’s argument that the trial court erred by
finding that he engaged in “abuse.” To obtain relief under RSA chapter 173-B,
a plaintiff must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the defendant
engaged in “abuse.” S.C. v. G.C., 175 N.H. 158, 163 (2022). A finding of
“abuse” requires proof that the defendant, who is a family or household
member or a current or former sexual or intimate partner of the plaintiff,
committed or attempted to commit one or more statutorily enumerated
criminal acts, and that such conduct constitutes “a credible present threat to
the [plaintiff’s] safety.” RSA 173-B:1, I; see S.C., 175 N.H. at 163. The trial
court “may consider evidence of such acts, regardless of their proximity in time
to the filing of the petition, which, in combination with recent conduct, reflects
an ongoing pattern of behavior which reasonably causes or has caused the
[plaintiff] to fear for his or her safety or well-being.” RSA 173-B:1, I.
On appeal, the trial court’s findings of fact are final, and we engage in de
novo review of questions of law raised by the appealing party. RSA 173-B:3, VI
(2022); S.C., 175 N.H. at 162. We review sufficiency of the evidence claims as a
matter of law and will uphold the trial court’s findings and rulings unless they
lack evidentiary support or are tainted by error of law. S.C., 175 N.H. at 162.
We view the evidence in the light most favorable to the prevailing party, here,
the plaintiff, deferring to the trial court’s judgment in evaluating witness
credibility and the weight of the evidence, id. at 162-63, recognizing that the
trial court has the benefit of observing the demeanor of the witnesses first-
hand, see State v. Giles, 140 N.H. 714, 718-19 (1996) (deferring to fact finder
on credibility determinations in part because “a trial transcript provides no
indication of a witness’s tone of voice or demeanor”). Indeed, the trial court is
not required to believe even uncontested testimony. See In the Matter of Aube
& Aube, 158 N.H. 459, 466 (2009).
In this case, the trial court found that the defendant engaged in the
following predicate acts for purposes of RSA 173-B:1, I: (1) “[c]riminal
threatening as defined in RSA 631:4,” RSA 173-B:1, I(b); and (2) “[d]estruction
of property as defined in RSA 634:1 and RSA 634:2,” RSA 173-B:1, I(e).
Specifically, the court found that the defendant engaged in such abuse when
he: (1) “threatened to kill the plaintiff on numerous occasions in 2024-2025”;
(2) “went to plaintiff’s home and looked in windows after he got a [statutory]
restraining order against her in Massachusetts”; (3) “broke [a] window screen in
2024”; and (4) “threw plaintiff’s phone damaging it and [the] wall.” These
actions, according to the trial court, “placed the plaintiff in fear for herself” and
“would place any reasonable person in a similar state of fear.”
In challenging the finding that he engaged in abuse, the defendant
argues that, because he had both sought and obtained a restraining order in
Massachusetts and had filed a parenting motion against the plaintiff before she
filed her petition, her petition was retaliatory, and the trial court erred by
crediting her testimony and finding that his conduct constituted a credible
present threat to her safety. He further argues that based on the facts testified
to by the plaintiff, going to her home and looking in her windows did not
constitute a crime enumerated under RSA 173-B:1, I.
As noted above, we defer to the trial court’s evaluation of the parties’
credibility. S.C., 175 N.H. at 162-63. Construed most favorably to the
plaintiff, see id., the evidence supports the trial court’s findings that: (1) the
defendant threatened to kill the plaintiff in 2024 and 2025, including a threat
approximately two weeks before she filed her petition; (2) the defendant broke a
window screen on the plaintiff’s house in 2024; (3) the defendant threw the
plaintiff’s phone damaging both the phone and a wall; and (4) these acts
caused the plaintiff reasonably to fear for her safety. In turn, this evidence
supports the trial court’s determinations that the defendant engaged in
criminal threatening, see RSA 631:4, I(d) (2016), and destruction of property,
see RSA 634:2, I (Supp. 2025), both enumerated crimes under the statute, see
RSA 173-B:1, I(b), (e), and that these acts constituted a credible present threat
to the plaintiff. Under these circumstances, it is immaterial whether going to
the plaintiff’s home and looking in her windows was not a predicate act of
abuse. See Kessler v. Gleich, 156 N.H. 488, 494 (2007) (stating that a
judgment will not be disturbed for error that did not affect outcome or cause a
party harm). On this record, the determination that the defendant engaged in
abuse was neither lacking evidentiary support nor tainted by error of law.
S.C., 175 N.H. at 162.

We next address whether the trial court erred by denying the defendant’s
motion to vacate the protective order without a hearing. Following his timely
appeal to this court, the defendant filed the motion to vacate in the trial court,
identifying four specific factual claims made by the plaintiff in her testimony
that, he asserted, were false. The plaintiff had specifically alleged each of the
four claims in her domestic violence petition. As to each claim, the defendant
described evidence that, he asserted, was inconsistent with the claim and
proved that the plaintiff had perjured herself, including recorded video or audio
evidence, written communications from the plaintiff or her attorney, transcripts
from other court proceedings, and police reports. The defendant argued that
the alleged perjury amounted to “fraud upon the court” sufficient to vitiate the
judgment. After we remanded the case to allow the trial court to rule on the
motion, the trial court denied it, relying in part on the fact that the defendant
possessed the evidence described in his motion at the time of the final hearing.
In substance, we construe the defendant’s motion as an untimely post-
decision motion for reconsideration or for a new trial. See Fam. Div. R. 1.26(F).
We review the trial court’s decision on such a motion for an unsustainable
exercise of discretion. See Walker v. Walker, 158 N.H. 602, 607 (2009);
Hodgdon v. Weeks Mem. Hosp., 128 N.H. 366, 368 (1986); Brigham v. Hudson
Motors, Inc., 118 N.H. 590, 593 (1978). Likewise, we review the trial court’s
decisions regarding whether to hold a hearing or accept new evidence on the
motion for an unsustainable exercise of discretion. See Smith v. Shepard, 144
N.H. 262, 265 (1999); Provencher v. Buzzell-Plourde Assoc., 142 N.H. 848, 851
(1998); see also Fam. Div. R. 1.26(F) (stating that “[a] hearing on [a] motion [for
reconsideration or other post-decision relief] shall not be permitted except by
order of the Court”). To establish an unsustainable exercise of discretion, the
defendant must demonstrate that the trial court’s ruling was clearly untenable
or unreasonable to the prejudice of his case. Walker, 158 N.H. at 607. Our
focus is on whether there is an objective basis sufficient to sustain the trial
court’s discretionary judgment. Id.
“Fraud on the court” generally does not encompass “garden-variety
fraud,” but rather is “that species of fraud which does or attempts to, defile the
court itself, or is a fraud perpetrated by officers of the court so that the judicial
machinery cannot perform in the usual manner its impartial task of adjudging
cases.” Conant v. O’Meara, 167 N.H. 644, 651 (2015) (quotations and
emphasis omitted); see also Bricker v. Sceva Speare Hosp., 115 N.H. 709, 711
(1975) (Bricker II) (stating that to warrant a new trial for fraud based on
allegedly perjured testimony, the alleged fraud “must lead to the conclusion
that the process of justice was thwarted or perverted by creating apparent
jurisdiction over the matter when none existed, or that the plaintiff was
prevented from contesting the issues involved”). Even when a claim of fraud
