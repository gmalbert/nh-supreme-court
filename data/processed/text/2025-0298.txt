THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0298, State of New Hampshire v. David W.
Powell, the court on September 28, 2026, issued the following
order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The defendant, David W. Powell, appeals his conviction of
harassment, RSA 644:4, I (Supp. 2025), following a jury trial in Superior Court
(English, J.). He argues that the trial court erred in admitting evidence of prior
bad acts pursuant to New Hampshire Rule of Evidence 404(b) for the purpose
of establishing motive. We affirm.
We review the trial court’s evidentiary rulings for an unsustainable
exercise of discretion and will reverse only if they were clearly untenable or
unreasonable to the prejudice of the defendant’s case. See State v. Nightingale,
160 N.H. 569, 573 (2010). Because the trial court ruled upon the admissibility
of the challenged evidence before trial, “we consider only what was presented at
the pretrial hearing.” Id. (quotation omitted).
The purpose of Rule 404(b) “is to ensure that an accused is tried on the
merits of the crime charged and to prevent a conviction that is based upon
propensity and character inferences drawn from evidence of other crimes or
wrongs.” State v. Tufano, 175 N.H. 662, 665 (2023) (quotation omitted). Rule
404(b) provides:
Evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is not admissible to prove
the character of a person in order to show that the person acted in
conformity therewith. It may, however, be admissible for other
purposes, such as proof of motive, opportunity, intent,
preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or
accident.
N.H. R. Ev. 404(b)(1). Evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is admissible
only if: (1) “it is relevant for a purpose other than proving the person’s
character or disposition”; (2) “there is clear proof, meaning that there is
sufficient evidence to support a finding by the fact-finder that the other crimes,
wrongs or acts occurred and that the person committed them”; and (3) “the
probative value of the evidence is not substantially outweighed by the danger of
unfair prejudice.” N.H. R. Ev. 404(b)(2). “[T]he State is required to specify the
purpose for which the evidence is offered and articulate the precise chain of
reasoning by which the proffered evidence will tend to prove or disprove an
issue actually in dispute, without relying upon forbidden inferences of
predisposition, character, or propensity.” State v. Clark, 174 N.H. 586, 593
(2021). The State bears the burden of demonstrating the admissibility of prior
bad acts. See Tufano, 175 N.H. at 665.
The defendant argues that the trial court erred in admitting evidence of
prior bad acts to prove motive. As an initial matter, it is not clear that the
defendant preserved this issue for review. “The general rule in this jurisdiction
is that a contemporaneous and specific objection is required to preserve an
issue for appellate review.” State v. Winstead, 150 N.H. 244, 246 (2003)
(quotation omitted). Prior to trial, defense counsel asked the court to give a
limiting instruction to the jury identifying the proper purposes for which the
evidence was being offered. The prosecutor identified the proper purposes as
motive and intent. The court stated that its instructions would include both
motive and intent, and defense counsel did not object.
However, even if we assume, without deciding, that the issue is
preserved, we conclude that the evidence was relevant and admissible to show
motive. The State charged the defendant with harassment and criminal
threatening. The State had the burden to prove that the defendant made the
threats toward the victim “[w]ith the purpose to annoy or alarm another,” see
RSA 644:4, I(e), and “with a purpose to terrorize any person,” see RSA 631:4,
I(d) (206). The defense argued that the defendant acts in “strange, alarming
ways,” but that the victim misunderstood these interactions. Here, as in
Brewster, “[t]he defendant’s prior threatening behavior towards [the victim] was
probative of his motive and intent towards [the victim] and also to [the victim’s]
own state of mind at the time of the charged offenses.” State v. Brewster, 147
N.H. 645, 649 (2002).
The defendant does not dispute that the prior bad act evidence was
admissible to show intent. The court instructed the jury that it “may not
consider that evidence for propensity purposes,” but only “for the limited
purpose of determining [the defendant’s] motive and intent related to the
charged offenses.” “[T]he trial court’s limiting instructions, which the jury is
presumed to follow, minimized the possibility that the jury would misuse the
evidence, thus reducing the potential for unfair prejudice.” State v. Gaudet,
166 N.H. 390, 397 (quotation omitted). We conclude that the probative value of
2
the evidence was not substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair
prejudice. See N.H. R. Ev. 404(b)(2).
Affirmed.
