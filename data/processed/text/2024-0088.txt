THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2024-0088, State of New Hampshire v. Zachary
Medlock; State of New Hampshire v. Heather Medlock, the court
on January 28, 2025, issued the following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the limited record
submitted on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this
order. See Sup. Ct. R. 20(2). The defendants, Zachary Medlock and Heather
Medlock, appeal their convictions in the Circuit Court (Derby, J.), following a
bench trial, on three counts of owning dogs considered to be a nuisance, a
menace, or vicious, see RSA 466:31 (2018), and one count of animal cruelty, see
RSA 644:8 (Supp. 2023). They argue that: (1) the evidence was insufficient to
support the convictions; (2) they should not have been charged under RSA 644:8;
and (3) the fines imposed were excessive. We affirm.
The defendants first argue that the evidence, while arguably sufficient to
show civil negligence, was insufficient to prove criminal negligence as required for
convictions under RSA 644:8, III. See State v. Butler, 175 N.H. 444, 447 (2022)
(mens rea required for animal cruelty under Paragraph III is criminal negligence).
It is the defendants’ burden to provide a record sufficient to decide their issues
on appeal. State v. Winward, 161 N.H. 533, 542 (2011); Sup. Ct. R. 15(3) (“If the
moving party intends to argue in the supreme court that a finding or conclusion
is unsupported by the evidence or is contrary to the evidence, the moving party
shall include in the record a transcript of all evidence relevant to such finding or
conclusion.”); see also Town of Nottingham v. Newman, 147 N.H. 131, 137 (2001)
(rules of appellate practice not relaxed for self-represented litigants). The
defendants failed to provide a transcript of the bench trial. Absent a transcript,
we must assume that the evidence was sufficient to support the trial court’s
findings. See Atwood v. Owens, 142 N.H. 396, 396 (1997). Accordingly, we
assume that the evidence was sufficient to prove that the defendants were
criminally negligent under RSA 644:8, III. See id.
The defendants next argue that they should not have been prosecuted for
animal cruelty under RSA 644:8 because the State’s concern was the victims of
the dogs’ attack, not the welfare of the dogs. The State counters that the
defendants failed to preserve this argument because they did not provide a record
sufficient to show that they raised it in the trial court. See State v. Perez, 173
N.H. 251, 258 (2020). Even if this argument were preserved, prosecutors have
broad discretion in bringing charges against an accused. State v. Gooden, 133
N.H. 674, 680 (1990). We assume the evidence was sufficient to support
convictions under both statutes. See Atwood, 142 N.H. at 396. On this record,
we cannot conclude that the prosecutor unsustainably exercised its discretion.
See Gooden, 133 N.H. at 680.
The defendants next argue that the trial court imposed fines that exceeded
the maximum fines allowed under RSA 466:31-a, II (2018). The State counters
that the defendants failed to preserve this argument because they did not provide
a record sufficient to show that they raised it in the trial court. See Perez, 173
N.H. at 258. Even if this argument were preserved, the fines listed on the police
department form cited by the defendants apply to persons paying a civil forfeiture
to avoid criminal prosecution. See RSA 466:31-a, II(a)-(d) (civil forfeiture
schedule). The trial court was not bound by the civil forfeiture schedule set forth
in RSA 466:31-a, II when sentencing the defendants. See RSA 466:31-a, I (2018).
Affirmed.
MacDonald, C.J., and Bassett, Donovan, and Countway, JJ., concurred.
