THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0502, In re Estate of Irene Turner; Wendy
Dowaliby v. Lisa Locke & a., the court on September 10, 2026,
issued the following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The appellant, Wendy Dowaliby, appeals an order issued by
the Circuit Court (Cross, J.), following an evidentiary hearing, denying her
motion to reexamine the 2023 will of the decedent, her mother, and denying
her verified petition to set aside an inter vivos trust created by the decedent in
2023. On appeal, the appellant argues that the trial court erred by: (1)
allegedly treating disputed material facts as resolved; (2) finding that the
decedent had capacity to execute the 2023 will and trust and a 2022 durable
power of attorney; (3) allegedly failing to address her allegations of undue
influence; (4) allegedly failing to address her claims that the trustee, Lisa
Locke, breached her fiduciary obligations and improperly handled trust funds;
(5) allegedly failing to enforce a discovery order; (6) finding that she lacked
standing to seek the trustee’s removal for trust mismanagement or breach of
fiduciary duty; and (7) allegedly failing to consider undue influence that she
claims the decedent’s counsel imposed. We affirm.
“The findings of fact of the judge of probate are final unless they are so
plainly erroneous that such findings could not be reasonably made.” RSA 567-
A:4 (2019). Accordingly, we will not disturb the trial court’s order unless it is
unsupported by the evidence or plainly erroneous as a matter of law. In re
Estate of Dow, 174 N.H. 37, 41 (2021). We defer to the trial court’s judgment
in resolving conflicts in testimony, assessing the credibility of witness, and
evaluating the weight of the evidence presented, mindful that the trial court is
in the best position to measure the persuasiveness and credibility of evidence
and is not required to believe even uncontested evidence. See In re Estate of
King, 151 N.H. 425, 429 (2004).
It is the appellant’s burden to provide a record that is sufficient to decide
the issues she is raising and to demonstrate that she raised those issues in the
trial court. See Bean v. Red Oak Prop. Mgmt., 151 N.H. 248, 250 (2004). We
assume that relevant portions of the trial court record not provided on appeal
support the trial court’s decision. See id. This rule applies to transcripts of
relevant hearings as well as relevant pleadings, exhibits, and trial court orders.
See id. (assuming that evidence supported the result reached by the trial court
in light of the appealing party’s failure to provide hearing transcript); In the
Matter of Silva & Silva, 171 N.H. 1, 12 (2018) (applying Bean to failure to
provide relevant court order); In the Matter of Rokowski & Rokowski, 168 N.H.
57, 62 (2015) (applying Bean to failure to provide exhibits admitted into
evidence at evidentiary hearing); Estate of Day v. Hanover Ins. Co., 162 N.H.
415, 422 (2011) (applying Bean to failure to provide relevant pleadings in the
trial court). Likewise, it is the appellant’s burden to establish reversible error.
See Gallo v. Traina, 166 N.H. 737, 740 (2014). These rules are not relaxed for
self-represented parties. See In the Matter of Birmingham & Birmingham, 154
N.H. 51, 56 (2006) (stating that self-represented parties are bound by the same
procedural rules that govern parties represented by counsel).
In this case, after carefully reviewing the extensive evidentiary record, the
trial court found that the decedent had testamentary capacity and was not
unduly influenced at the time she executed her 2023 will and trust. With
respect to the appellant’s claims seeking to remove the trustee on grounds that
the trustee mismanaged trust assets and breached her fiduciary obligations,
the trial court determined not only that the appellant lacked standing to
remove the trustee, but that the appellant’s claims, on their merits, had “no
credible support in the evidence.” In finding that the appellant’s claims
seeking to remove the trustee lacked credible evidentiary support, the trial
court noted that it had considered not only the evidence submitted at the final
hearing, but evidence submitted at an April 11, 2025 hearing on a motion for a
temporary restraining order, a power of attorney accounting, a trust
accounting, and a response filed by the trustee on March 12, 2025. We note
that the appellant has not provided a transcript of the April 11, 2025
temporary restraining order hearing, the power of attorney and trust
accountings referenced by the trial court, or the pleading filed by the trustee on
March 12, 2025. Nor has she provided copies of her motion to reexamine the
2023 will, her verified petition to set aside the trust, or the discovery order that
she claims the trial court failed to enforce. Accordingly, we assume these
documents support the trial court’s findings and rulings that the appellant is
now challenging. See Bean, 151 N.H. at 250.
Based upon our review of the trial court’s well-reasoned order, the
appellant’s arguments, the relevant law, and the portions of the record that the
appellant has provided, including the transcript of the seven-hour final
2
hearing, we conclude that the appellant has not demonstrated that the trial
court’s order is unsupported by the evidence or plainly erroneous as a matter
of law. See Estate of Dow, 174 N.H. at 41; Gallo, 166 N.H. at 740.
Affirmed.
Donovan, Countway, Gould, and Will, JJ., concurred.
