THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2024-0306, State of New Hampshire v. Joseph
James Nugent, III, the court on February 19, 2025, issued the
following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The defendant, Joseph James Nugent, III, appeals orders of
the Circuit Court (Subers, J.), denying his motion to vacate the administrative
entry of a finding of guilty on a speeding violation based upon his failure to
attend a pretrial conference, see RSA 265:60 (2024); RSA 502-A:19-b, II(b)
(2020), and denying his motion to reconsider the ruling on the motion to
vacate. On appeal, the defendant raises a number of arguments challenging
these rulings, claiming that he was never notified of the pretrial conference,
and that the trial court’s rulings, therefore, violated his State and Federal Due
Process rights. We affirm in part and dismiss the appeal in part.
The record on appeal reflects that the trial court entered its
administrative finding of guilty on the speeding charge and provided notice to
the defendant of the finding and resulting fine on February 29, 2024. The
defendant subsequently moved to vacate the finding on March 29, asserting
that the court failed to provide notice of the pretrial conference, and that its
failure to provide notice was intentional and retaliatory for requesting a hearing
and for prior events. We note that the record on appeal does not reflect that
the defendant supported the motion with an affidavit or acknowledgment that
making a false statement may subject him to criminal penalties. See Dist. Div.
R. 1.8(B) (“The Court will not hear any motion grounded upon facts, unless the
moving party indicates in writing an understanding that making a false
statement in the pleading may subject that party to criminal penalties, or the
facts are apparent from the record or from the papers on file in the case, or are
agreed to and stated in writing signed by the parties or their attorneys . . . .”).
By notice of decision dated April 8, 2024, the trial court denied the
motion to vacate, finding that notice of the February 29 hearing was mailed on
December 26, 2023, and that the mailing was not returned to the court. The
record on appeal supports these findings. Sixteen days later, on April 24, the
defendant filed an untimely motion to reconsider the denial of his motion to
vacate. See Dist. Div. R. 1.8(I) (motion to reconsider must be filed “within 10
days of the date on the written notice of the order or decision”). In the motion,
the defendant repeated some of the factual allegations in his motion to vacate,
and asserted that neither he nor his sister, who he claimed to be the “only
holder of the key to” his post office box, received the hearing notice. As with
the motion to vacate, the record on appeal does not reflect that the defendant
supported the motion to reconsider with an affidavit or acknowledgment that
making a false statement may subject him to criminal penalties. See Dist. Div.
R. 1.8(B). The trial court denied the motion to reconsider by notice of decision
dated May 2, 2024, and the defendant filed the present appeal on May 31,
2024.
At the outset, we note that, because the defendant did not timely move to
reconsider the denial of his motion to vacate the finding of guilty, and because
the trial court did not expressly waive the untimeliness of the motion to
reconsider within the appeal period governing the earlier denial of the motion
to vacate, the appeal period governing the earlier order was not stayed. See
Sup. Ct. R. 7(1)(C) (providing that untimely post-decision motion does not stay
running of 30-day appeal period unless trial court waives untimeliness within
appeal period, and that absent express waiver of untimeliness within appeal
period, the trial court’s ruling on untimely post-decision motion does not
extend appeal period); see also Germain v. Germain, 137 N.H. 82, 85 (1993)
(stating that merely accepting and ruling on an untimely motion to reconsider
is not a waiver of untimeliness within the appeal period). Accordingly, the
appeal is timely only as to the May 2, 2024 decision denying the untimely
motion to reconsider, and is otherwise dismissed. See Sup. Ct. R. 5(4);
Germain, 137 N.H. at 84-85 (treating appeal from denial of untimely motion for
reconsideration as timely appeal only as to the denial of the motion, and not as
to the earlier decision on the merits); In re Estate of Porter, 159 N.H. 212, 214
(2009) (citing Germain for same proposition).
We will uphold the trial court’s decision on a motion for reconsideration
absent an unsustainable exercise of discretion. State v. Barkus, 152 N.H. 701,
705 (2005). To establish an unsustainable exercise of discretion, the defendant
must demonstrate that the trial court’s decision was clearly untenable or
unreasonable to the prejudice of his case. State v. Voight, 176 N.H. 763, 766
(2024), 2024 N.H. 46, at ¶8. Here, in addition to the untimeliness of the
motion, we note that “[t]here is a presumption that a properly addressed mailed
communication has been received in the absence of other evidence to the
contrary,” Cote v. Cote, 123 N.H. 376 (1983), that application of this
presumption generally satisfies due process, see Appeal of City of Concord, 161
N.H. 169, 173-74 (2010), and that the defendant’s claims not to have received
notice of the pretrial hearing were unsupported by affidavit.
As the appealing party, the defendant has the burden of demonstrating
that the trial court erred. Gallo v. Traina, 166 N.H. 737, 740 (2014). Based
upon our review of the record, the defendant’s arguments, and the relevant
law, we conclude that the defendant has not demonstrated that the denial of
his untimely motion to reconsider was clearly untenable or unreasonable to the
prejudice of his case. See id.; Voight, 176 N.H. at 766, 2024 N.H. 46, at ¶8.

Finally, to the extent the defendant is arguing that the trial court was
biased against him, he has not established that a reasonable person would
have questioned the impartiality of the trial court. See Sup. Ct. R. 38, Canon
2.11; State v. Bader, 148 N.H. 265, 268 (2002).
Affirmed in part; and
dismissed in part.
