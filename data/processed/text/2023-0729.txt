THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2023-0729, State of New Hampshire v. Ghenton
Doty, the court on March 25, 2025, issued the following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The defendant, Ghenton Doty, appeals his conviction,
following a jury trial in Superior Court (Colburn, J.), on a charge of bail
jumping for knowingly failing to appear at a court hearing as required by the
conditions of his release. See RSA 642:8, I(a) (2016). He argues that the
evidence was insufficient to support the jury’s finding that he acted knowingly
when he failed to appear at the relevant hearing. We reverse.
A challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence raises a question of law that
we review de novo. State v. Seibel, 174 N.H. 440, 445 (2021). When
considering a challenge to the sufficiency of evidence to support a conviction,
we objectively review the entire record, construing the evidence, and all
reasonable inferences from it, in the light most favorable to the State, and
determine whether any rational trier of fact could have found guilt beyond a
reasonable doubt. Id. We examine each item of evidence in the context of the
entire case, not in isolation. Id. The trier of fact is entitled to draw reasonable
inferences from the facts proved as well as from facts found as the result of
other inferences so long as they can reasonably be drawn therefrom. See id.
The defendant bears the burden of demonstrating that the evidence was
insufficient to prove guilt. State v. Saintil-Brown, 172 N.H. 110, 117 (2019).
When the evidence as to one or more of the elements is solely circumstantial,
the defendant must establish that the evidence does not exclude all reasonable
conclusions except guilt. Id. The proper analysis is not whether every possible
conclusion consistent with innocence has been excluded, but whether all
reasonable conclusions based on the evidence have been excluded. Id. When
solely circumstantial evidence is at issue, “the critical question is whether, even
assuming all credibility resolutions in favor of the State, the inferential chain of
circumstances is of sufficient strength that guilt is the sole rational
conclusion.” Seibel, 174 N.H. at 445 (quotation omitted).
To convict the defendant in this case, the State was required to prove
that, following his pretrial release in a criminal matter, he “knowingly fail[ed] to
appear before a court as required by the conditions of his release.” RSA 642:8,
I(a). Specifically, the State was required to prove that the defendant knowingly
failed to appear at a show cause hearing held in the superior court on March
30, 2022, as required by bail conditions in connection with a criminal matter.
The evidence establishes that on November 22, 2019, the defendant was
accused of certain criminal conduct, following which his then-spouse told him
to leave their home. On November 27, 2019, when the defendant returned to
the home and collected personal belongings, police were dispatched to the
home on a complaint of an “[u]nwanted subject,” and the defendant told an
officer that he had returned to the home against an attorney’s advice, that the
criminal allegations were going to ruin his life, and that he was distraught. On
December 6, 2019, an attorney, who was not the defendant’s attorney at the
time of trial, entered an appearance on the defendant’s behalf in superior court
on his pending criminal charges. On January 9, 2020, the trial court issued
an order setting personal recognizance bail subject to a condition requiring
that he “appear at all court proceedings as ordered.”
