THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2024-0079, In the Matter of Ryan Green and
Danielle Green, the court on December 6, 2024, issued the
following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(2). The petitioner, Ryan Green (husband), appeals the final
decree of the Circuit Court (Derby, J.), following a hearing, in his divorce from
the respondent, Danielle Green (wife). He argues that the trial court erred in
dividing equally the equity in the marital home and the debt on a recreational
vehicle. We affirm.
The husband first argues that the trial court erred in dividing equally the
equity in the marital home. We afford the trial court broad discretion in
determining matters of property distribution when fashioning a final divorce
decree. In the Matter of Sanborn & Bart, 174 N.H. 343, 353 (2021). “In a
divorce proceeding, marital property is not to be divided by some mechanical
formula but in a manner deemed ‘just’ based upon the evidence presented and
the equities of the case.” In the Matter of Letendre & Letendre, 149 N.H. 31, 35
(2002). We will not overturn the trial court’s decision absent an unsustainable
exercise of discretion or an error of law. In the Matter of Sanborn, 174 N.H. at
353. If the court’s findings can reasonably be made on the evidence, they will
stand. Id.
The parties met and started dating in November 2017, when the wife was
separated but not yet divorced from her prior husband. In 2018, the parties
started searching for a home together. On or about April 3, 2018, the husband
purchased the house that became their marital residence. The court found
that neither party paid significant funds toward the purchase of the property.
Shortly after the closing, the parties opened a joint bank account and started
making equal, $1,400 monthly contributions to the account. The parties used
the account to pay joint expenses, including the monthly mortgage payments
and regular household bills.
The wife’s divorce from her prior husband became final in 2018, and the
parties were married on July 27, 2019. In October 2020, the husband added
the wife to the deed of the marital home, as part of a refinancing. The wife
testified that, during the marriage, and while the husband was deployed
overseas, she provided her own labor and paid for materials to improve the
property. Her work included repairing, retiling, and decorating a bathroom,
repainting the basement, wallpapering other rooms, and replacing the front
door. In September 2022, the wife moved out and stopped making regular
contributions to the joint bank account. On October 3, 2022, when the
husband filed for divorce, the parties had been married for more than three
years.
The husband argues that he should receive a greater share of the equity
in the marital residence because: (1) the wife started contributing to the
mortgage and household expenses before the divorce from her prior husband
became final; (2) the husband purchased the home more than one year before
the parties were married; (3) their marriage was “short-term” (three years and
two months); (4) the husband allegedly contributed more toward home
improvements; and (5) the wife reduced the amount of her monthly
contributions toward the property after June 2022.
Under RSA 458:16-a, II (2018), an equal division of property is presumed
equitable. In the Matter of Silva & Silva, 171 N.H. 1, 11 (2018). RSA 458:16-a,
I (2018) “does not classify property based upon when or by whom it was
acquired, but rather assumes that all property is susceptible to division.” In
the Matter of Crowe & Crowe, 148 N.H. 218, 221 (2002). The trial court may
consider premarital cohabitation when formulating an equitable distribution of
marital property. In the Matter of Munson & Beal, 169 N.H. 274, 276 (2016).
Although it may be easier to return parties in a short-term marriage to their
premarital financial positions, it may not always be equitable to treat a short-
term marriage differently from a long-term marriage. Id. at 278. This is
especially true ‘[w]here the parties have invested money and effort for years in
an asset like a family business or house.” Rahn v. Rahn, 123 N.H. 222, 225
(1983).
The trial court found that, “when considering both monetary
contributions and uncompensated personal labor, there is not a significant
disparity between the parties’ contributions to the maintenance, management
and improvement of the marital residence from its acquisition through
September 2022.” The court found that September 2022 would be the
appropriate valuation date, rather than a later date, given that the husband
paid all costs relating to the home after the wife moved out. The court found
that the fair market value of the home in September 2022 was $496,980, and
that the mortgage balance on that date was $320,177.03. The court calculated
the wife’s one-half share of the $176,802.97 in equity to be $88,401.50.
The trial court found that “to the extent that [the] husband can prove a
greater financial outlay toward improvements, he has not presented any
evidence of how his greater financial outlays increased the value of the home
substantially more than the work [the] wife performed.” The court noted that
the wife reduced her payments to the joint bank account from $1,400 per
2
month to $700 per month in July, August, and September 2002. However, the
court found that the wife’s $700 monthly payments were “more than enough to
service the monthly interest and escrows, which would be $594.58 per month,”
and that “[f]urther adjustments would not be equitable.” Contrary to the
husband’s arguments, the court did not find that the wife’s reduced payments
were sufficient to cover half of the monthly mortgage payment, including
principal. We conclude that the record establishes an objective basis sufficient
to sustain the trial court’s decision to divide the equity in the marital home
equally. See Sanborn, 174 N.H at 353.
The husband next argues that the trial court erred in dividing equally the
debt relating to the parties’ recreational vehicle. The trial court has discretion
to allocate responsibility for debts in the manner it deems equitable. See In the
Matter of Costa & Costa, 156 N.H. 323, 327-28 (2007) (affirming trial court’s
assignment of approximately $252,000 in debt to petitioner that might
otherwise have been assignable in whole or in part to respondent). We will not
overturn the trial court’s decision absent an unsustainable exercise of
discretion. Id. at 326.
The court found that the loan balance on the vehicle as of September
2023 was $48,718.96, and the vehicle’s value as of that date was $35,835,
resulting in a $12,883.96 debt. The court found credible the wife’s testimony
that the husband would like to trade the vehicle for another “more to his
liking.” Accordingly, the court ordered that the husband could either deduct
$6,442 (half the calculated debt) or half the deficiency remaining on the loan
after a third-party sale from the equity payment to the wife for the marital
home. Based upon our review of the record, we cannot conclude that the trial
court unsustainably exercised its discretion in dividing equally the debt
relating to the parties’ recreational vehicle. See id.
To the extent that the husband’s brief may contain other arguments, we
conclude that they are inadequately developed, see Blackmer, 149 N.H. 47, 49
(2003), and decline to address them, see Vogel v. Vogel, 137 N.H. 321, 322
(1993).
Affirmed.
MacDonald, C.J., and Bassett, Donovan, and Countway, JJ., concurred.
