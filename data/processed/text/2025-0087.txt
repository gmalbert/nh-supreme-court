THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0087, State of New Hampshire v. Stanley
Watson, the court on September 1, 2026, issued the following
order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted on
appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See Sup. Ct.
R. 20(3). The defendant, Stanley Watson, appeals his convictions, following a
jury trial in Superior Court (Delker, J.), on three counts of aggravated felonious
sexual assault. See RSA 632-A:2 (Supp. 2025) (amended 2026). On appeal, the
defendant argues that his convictions should be reversed, or that he should be
granted a new trial, because the trial court erred in overruling his objection to the
State’s opening statement in which he contends the prosecutor made remarks
that infringed upon his State and Federal Constitutional right to remain silent
and improperly shifted the burden of proof onto him. We affirm.
Before addressing the substance of the defendant’s arguments, we first
note that because he did not mention the State Constitution in his objection at
trial, either generally or by citing a specific provision, we analyze his arguments
only under the Federal Constitution. See State v. Fowler, 132 N.H. 540, 545
(1989); State v. Dellorfano, 128 N.H. 628, 632-33 (1986).
“We will not overturn the trial court’s decision on whether a mistrial or
other remedial action is necessary absent an unsustainable exercise of
discretion.” State v. Gaudet, 166 N.H. 390, 397 (2014) (quotation omitted). “The
standard for reversible error in a prosecutor’s opening statement is that the
prosecutor must be shown to have acted in bad faith, the opening statement
must be completely unsupported by the evidence, and the defendant must be
prejudiced thereby.” Id. at 397-98 (quotation and emphasis omitted).
The threshold question, however, is whether the prosecutor’s remarks were
improper. See id. at 397-98, 401. “A defendant’s decision not to testify or
present evidence in his own defense can provide no basis for an adverse
comment by the prosecutor.” Fowler, 132 N.H. at 545. When determining
whether the remarks constituted improper commentary upon the defendant’s
right to remain silent, we consider “whether the language used was manifestly
intended or was of such a character that the jury would naturally and necessarily
take it to be a comment on the failure of the accused to testify.” State v. Merrill,
125 N.H. 479, 480 (1984) (quotations omitted). When determining whether the
remarks improperly shifted the burden of proof onto the defendant, we consider
“whether the prosecutor’s comments were unfair and so infected the entire trial
as to deprive the defendant of due process of law.” State v. Parker, 142 N.H. 319,
322 (1997).
In this case, the defendant objected to the following remarks made by the
prosecutor in his opening statement:
Regardless, we anticipate the defense to try and attack [the
victims’] credibility, to emphasize these alleged sexual abuses
occurred nearly 25 years ago with neither [victim] coming forward
until they were adults, to point out any inconsistencies that there
may be from any statements [the victims] made before coming to this
court and what they testify to at trial, to paint [the victims] as having
ulterior motives to making these allegations, that there is money
involved from a civil case they have against the state agency charged
with their care as children, and that they have taken from — taken
money from a fund associated with that civil case.
Credibility is a central issue in this trial. But make no
mistake, two grown men talking about some of the most tragic, most
horrific, most embarrassing details of their lives before a room of
many strangers is not something that can be bought, especially in
this case because there is no money to be gained in this case.
Defense may try to merge the civil case that [the victims] are
involved in with this criminal trial, but that is an entirely separate
proceeding. [The victims] do not have to testify in this case to move
their civil matter forward. No amount of money can make a person
feel comfortable to talk about sex acts they believe that this
Defendant had made them believe as children that [sic] had to do,
had to participate in. But they will testify before you nonetheless,
not because they want money, but because they want justice.
Although the defendant contends that these remarks “violated [his] right to
remain silent by informing the jury what they should expect from the defense
during the . . . course of the trial,” and “improperly suggest[ed] that [he] has the
burden to produce evidence,” we disagree. The prosecutor’s opening remarks did
not in any way shift the burden of proof onto the defendant. See Parker, 142
N.H. at 321-23. Nor did they even allude to the defendant’s right to remain
silent. See Merrill, 125 N.H. at 479-80. Thus, we conclude that the trial court
2
did not unsustainably exercise its discretion in overruling the defendant’s
objection to the State’s opening statement.1 See Gaudet, 166 N.H. at 397-98.
Accordingly, the defendant’s convictions are affirmed.
Affirmed.
Donovan, Countway, and Will, JJ., concurred.

Clerk
