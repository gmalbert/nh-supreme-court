THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2026-0361, O.R. v. A.B., the court on
September 28, 2026, issued the following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The defendant, A.B., appeals a civil stalking final order of
protection issued by the Circuit Court (Collins, J.), following an evidentiary
hearing, in favor of the plaintiff, O.R. See RSA 633:3-a, III-a (2016). The
defendant argues, among other things, that the evidence was insufficient to
support the trial court’s finding that the course of conduct engaged in by the
defendant caused the plaintiff reasonable fear for the plaintiff’s personal safety
or the safety of a member of the plaintiff’s immediate family. We reverse.
Any stalking victim “may seek relief by filing a civil petition in the
[circuit] court”; upon proof by a preponderance of the evidence, the court “shall
grant such relief as is necessary to bring about a cessation of stalking.” Id.
“Stalking” includes “[p]urposely, knowingly, or recklessly engag[ing] in a course
of conduct targeted at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person
to fear for his or her personal safety or the safety of a member of that person’s
immediate family, and the person is actually placed in such fear.” RSA 633:3-
a, I(a) (2016). “‘Course of conduct’ means 2 or more acts over a period of time,
however short, which evidences a continuity of purpose.” RSA 633:3-a, II(a)
(2016). Acts that may constitute a “course of conduct” include, but are not
limited to, “[a]ny act of communication, as defined in RSA 644:4, II.” RSA
633:3-a, II(a)(7). Under RSA 644:4, II (Supp. 2025), “communicates” means “to
impart a message by any method of transmission.”
The trial court’s “findings of facts shall be final, but questions of law may
be transferred from the circuit court to the supreme court.” RSA 173-B:3, VI
(2022); see RSA 633:3-a, III-a (providing that, in civil stalking proceedings, “the
procedures and burdens of proof to be applied . . . shall be the same as those
set forth in RSA 173-B”). We review sufficiency of the evidence claims as a
matter of law and will uphold the trial court’s findings and rulings unless they
lack evidentiary support or are tainted by error of law. Despres v. Hampsey,
162 N.H. 398, 401 (2011). We view the evidence in the light most favorable to a
prevailing plaintiff, deferring to the trial court’s evaluation of the witnesses’
credibility. See id. at 401, 404.
The plaintiff filed civil stalking petitions against the defendant and two
other persons, alleging that all three persons engaged in a pattern of
harassment through text messages that “mock[ed]” or “ma[de] fun” of the
plaintiff, harassing phone calls, and causing companies to mail catalogs to the
plaintiff but substituting a mocking name for the plaintiff’s name as the
addressee. The harassment continued even after the plaintiff asked the three
parties to stop, notified them that law enforcement had been contacted, and
filed stalking petitions. With respect to fear, the plaintiff testified,
So with the packages, it’s — it’s messing with knowing, like, what
is, like, real and what’s, like, not real, like a scam. And it’s just — I
also don’t know what’s going to be in the packages either, so it’s
just, like, I just fear of, like, being harassed at my own house
where I have to go, like, home to every day. So that’s it, Your
Honor.
In granting a civil stalking final order of protection, the trial court found
that the defendant “[p]urposely, knowingly, or recklessly engaged in a
course of conduct targeted at” the plaintiff when the defendant
conspired with two other individuals to harass the plaintiff by
sending text messages, making phone calls and sending unwanted
catalogues to the plaintiff’s residence and the harassment
continued after the defendant was aware the police and court were
involved. The plaintiff was afraid of what [the plaintiff] would find
in [the plaintiff’s] mailbox each day.
On this record, we conclude that the trial court’s finding that the plaintiff
reasonably feared for the plaintiff’s personal safety or the safety of a member of
the plaintiff’s immediate family lacked evidentiary support. See id. at 401. For
a course of conduct to amount to “stalking” under RSA 633:3-a, I(a), it must do
more than cause the plaintiff to fear “being harassed at [the plaintiff’s] own
house.” Rather, it must cause the plaintiff to fear for the plaintiff’s “personal
safety or the safety of a member of that person’s immediate family.” RSA
633:3-a, I(a). Moreover, the fear must be objectively “reasonable.” Here, even
when the evidence is viewed most favorably to the plaintiff, the plaintiff testified
only to fearing further harassment at home and described, at most, a pattern of
harassment, but nothing that would cause a reasonable person to fear for that
person’s personal safety or the safety of an immediate family member.
We note that the unavailability of a civil stalking order of protection
under RSA 633:3-a does not necessarily leave a victim of harassment in the
position of the plaintiff with no remedy. Specifically, the superior court has
jurisdiction to restrain unwanted contact in the absence of an adequate remedy
at law, such as the unavailability of a civil stalking protective order. See State
v. Simone, 151 N.H. 328, 331 (2004); RSA 498:1 (Supp. 2025). Because the
2
evidence does not support the trial court’s finding of reasonable fear, we
reverse the civil stalking protective order issued in this case.
Reversed.
Donovan, Countway, Gould, and Will, JJ., concurred.
