THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0007, In the Matter of Amberlynn Curtis
and Clifton Boyd, the court on September 25, 2025, issued the
following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted on
appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See Sup. Ct.
R. 20(3). The respondent, Clifton Boyd (Father), appeals a decision of the Circuit
Court (Carroll, R., approved by Boyle, J.), issued following a hearing, that
resulted in an order directing Father to pay Amberlynn Curtis (Mother) a child
support arrearage of $18,772.40. We affirm.
The parties have four minor children. In 2016, Mother registered a foreign
support decree in New Hampshire and obtained a default uniform support order
(USO) against Father, who did not attend the hearing. Due to Father’s failure to
participate, the initial 2016 USO established a payment amount based on
paystubs provided by Mother that demonstrated Father’s regular, salaried pay
from his employer. Based on evidence that Father also earned income from
other sources, the 2016 USO provided that: “Obligor shall pay 40% of any
additional income he may receive when conducting his independent, ‘under-the-
table’ work[.]”1 From 2016 to 2019, Father typically paid child support through
wage garnishment; he did not report to Mother, or pay to her, a portion of any
income earned through other work.
Mother subsequently learned that Father had changed employers, and she
later received tax documentation that revealed Father’s total reported incomes for
years 2016-2019. The tax documents reflected earnings that exceeded the
estimated salary upon which the trial court based the 2016 support payment.
Accordingly, Mother petitioned for contempt, arguing Father failed to update her
on his change of employment, as required by the 2016 Order, and further failed
to pay her 40 percent of his additional earnings.
Given the ambiguity of the phrase “under the table,” the trial court
declined to hold Father in contempt. However, the trial court also determined
that Father failed to pay a portion of additional income earned as required by the
2016 Order, and it therefore enforced the order by requiring payment of an
$18,772.40 arrearage, which was the maximum amount Mother requested at the
final hearing. The trial court noted that although the use of the phrase “under

On appeal Father argues primarily that the trial court erred by: (1)
imposing the arrearage while simultaneously not finding him in contempt; and
(2) interpreting “under the table” work in the 2016 USO to include taxable

Trial courts have broad discretion in reviewing child support orders. In the
Matter of Greenberg & Greenberg, 174 N.H. 168, 171–72 (2021). They are in the
best position to determine the parties’ respective needs and their respective
ability to meet those needs. Id. We will not disturb the trial court’s rulings
regarding child support absent an unsustainable exercise of discretion or an
error of law. In the Matter of Ndyaija & Ndyaija, 173 N.H. 127, 140 (2020). We
review only whether the record establishes an objective basis sufficient to sustain
the discretionary judgment made, and we will not disturb the trial court’s
determination if it could reasonably have been made. In the Matter of Summers
& Summers, 172 N.H. 474, 479 (2019). Our standard of review is not whether
we would rule differently than the trial court, but whether a reasonable person
could have reached the same decision as the trial court based upon the same
evidence. Id. We will not substitute our judgment for that of the trial court. Id.
Nor will we reweigh the equities. Id.
Here, the trial court engaged in a thorough review and discussion of the
exhibits Mother and Father submitted at the hearing. However, Father has not
provided this court with copies of any exhibits, or copies of certain prior orders in
this case, including the 2016 Narrative. “It is the burden of the appealing party .
. . to provide this court with a record sufficient to decide [the] issues on appeal . .
. .” Bean v. Red Oak Prop. Mgmt., 151 N.H. 248, 250 (2004); see In the Matter of
Silva & Silva, 171 N.H. 1, 12 (2018) (applying Bean to failure to provide relevant
trial court orders); In the Matter of Rokowski & Rokowski, 168 N.H. 57, 62 (2015)
(applying Bean to failure to provide exhibits entered into evidence at evidentiary
hearing). Without these necessary portions of the record, we are required to
presume that “the evidence supports the trial court’s findings.” See Silverstein v.
Town of Alexandria, 150 N.H. 679, 681 (2004).
Upon review of the record before us, consisting of the transcript of the July
25, 2024 hearing, the trial court’s September 22, 2025 final order, and the 2016
USO, we conclude that there exists an objective basis sufficient to sustain the
discretionary judgments made by the trial court. Summers, 172 N.H. at 479.

Although Father argues that the 2016 USO cannot be reasonably construed to
require him to pay Mother 40 percent of his additional taxable income, the lower
court expressly interpreted the 2016 Narrative to include 40 percent of “income
which was additional to the reported salaried income from” his employer.2
Without a copy of the 2016 Narrative referenced by the trial court, we cannot
conclude that it erroneously interpreted the 2016 Order.
Finally, “child support payments are judgments when they are due and
payable.” In the Matter of Cole & Ford, 156 N.H. 609, 610 (2007). Therefore, the
family division has broad authority to enforce its child support orders. See In the
Matter of Hampers & Hampers, 166 N.H. 422, 442 (2014); see also RSA 490-D:3
(2010) (“[T]he judicial branch family division shall have the powers of a court of
equity . . . .”); RSA 490-F:3 (Supp. 2024) (granting circuit court the “jurisdiction,
powers, and duties conferred upon the former . . . judicial branch family
division”). Although the trial court did not deem Father’s noncompliance with the
additional earnings requirement to be willful, and thus contemptuous, the trial
court retained the authority to find that Father had nonetheless failed to comply
with a valid judgment and to enforce compliance with that judgment through the
payment of an arrearage.
With regard to the remaining issues presented by Father, we find that they
do not warrant further discussion. See Vogel v. Vogel, 137 N.H. 321, 322 (1993).
Accordingly, the trial court’s decision is affirmed.
Affirmed.
