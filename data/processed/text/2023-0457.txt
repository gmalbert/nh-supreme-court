THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2023-0457, State of New Hampshire v.
Montriville Graham, the court on March 25, 2025, issued the
following order:
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The defendant, Montriville Graham, appeals his conviction,
following a jury trial in Superior Court (Messer, J.), on nine charges of
aggravated felonious sexual assault for conduct occurring in 1993, see RSA
632-A:2 (Supp. 1993) (amended 1994, 1995, 1997, 1998, 1999, 2003, 2008,
2012, 2014, 2017, 2018, 2020), and thirteen charges of felonious sexual
assault for conduct occurring between 1989 and 1994, see RSA 632-A:3 (1986)
(amended 1997, 2003, 2006, 2008, 2010, 2014, 2017, 2020). On appeal, the
defendant argues that the Trial Court (Anderson, J.) may have erred by
determining, upon its in camera review of confidential records, that the
defendant was not entitled to disclosure of all the records that the trial court
reviewed. See State v. Hodges, 176 N.H. 751, 756 (2024), 2024 N.H. 44, ¶¶15-
16; State v. Girard, 173 N.H. 619, 628-29 (2020). We affirm.
In its memorandum of law, the State contends that, because the
defendant asserts only that the trial court “may have erred” by not disclosing
all of the records that it reviewed, and has not explained how the trial court in
fact unsustainably exercised its discretion, we should decline to review the
records that the trial court reviewed. In the absence of either access to the
records that the trial court reviewed or an error of law appearing on the face of
the trial court’s written order, however, it would be impossible for the
defendant to assert anything more than that the trial court “may have erred” by
not disclosing confidential records. Moreover, it is well established that “[w]hen
a defendant argues on appeal that a trial court’s ruling regarding the
disclosure of privileged records is unsustainable, we must review the same
records and determine whether the ruling was clearly unreasonable or
untenable to the prejudice of his case.” Hodges, 176 N.H. at 757, 2024 N.H.
44, ¶18; see also, e.g., State v. Warren, 177 N.H. ___, ___ (2025), 2025 N.H. 5,
¶46; State v. Paul, 176 N.H. 262, 270 (2023); State v. Racette, 175 N.H. 132,
141-42 (2022); State v. Soulia, 174 N.H. 225, 238-39 (2021); State v. Aldrich,
169 N.H. 345, 354 (2016); State v. Alwardt, 164 N.H. 52, 58 (2012).
Accordingly, we reject the State’s contention that the defendant’s failure
to explain how the trial court’s decisions to withhold confidential records from
disclosure were unsustainable amounts to a waiver of the right to challenge
those decisions. Having reviewed the records that the trial court examined in
camera, we conclude that the trial court’s decisions regarding those records
were neither clearly unreasonable nor untenable to the prejudice of the
defendant’s case. See Hodges, 176 N.H. at 757, 2024 N.H. 44, ¶ 18.
Affirmed.
Bassett, Donovan, and Countway, JJ., concurred.
