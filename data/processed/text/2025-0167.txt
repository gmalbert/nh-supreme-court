COUNTWAY, J.
[¶1] The State appeals an order of the Superior Court (Ogden, J.)
granting the defendant Timothy J. Hefferon’s motion to suppress evidence
obtained during a traffic stop. On appeal, the State argues that the trial court
erred when it concluded that the police did not have reasonable suspicion
sufficient to conduct the traffic stop. We reverse and remand.
[¶2] The following facts are taken from the trial court’s order granting the
defendant’s motion to suppress or are undisputed. In June 2023, the
Seabrook Police Department was investigating reports of drug sales occurring
at a specific apartment complex in Seabrook (the Seabrook Apartments). Prior
to that time, police had received several complaints or tips from residents of the
Seabrook Apartments related to this investigation. One specific report stated
that drug sales involving a red Chevrolet Cruze (the Cruze) and a silver Honda
Civic would regularly occur at the Seabrook Apartments. This report provided
the license plate numbers for both vehicles. Based on this information, police
identified the registered owner of the Cruze as a woman residing at the
Seabrook Apartments. Police had previously received reports that this
individual was involved in the sale of drugs at the Seabrook Apartments.
[¶3] On June 2, 2023, during the ongoing investigation into drug activity
at the Seabrook Apartments, officers with the Seabrook Police Department
observed the Cruze in the parking lot of a store in Seabrook. One officer1
observed two occupants in the Cruze: a male passenger, and a female driver,
whom the officer presumed was the registered owner of the vehicle and the
subject of the previous reports about drug dealing at the Seabrook Apartments.
[¶4] The officer surveilled the Cruze and followed it from the parking lot
in Seabrook to a specific apartment complex in Methuen, Massachusetts (the
Methuen Apartments). The officer was aware of an ongoing investigation into
drug activity at the Methuen Apartments. She was also aware that confidential
informants had previously purchased drugs in Seabrook that originated from
the Methuen Apartments.
[¶5] The officer did not follow the Cruze into the parking lot at the
Methuen Apartments because she could not do so covertly. Therefore, the
officer did not observe what the Cruze or its occupants did while in the parking
lot at the Methuen Apartments. The officer waited at a nearby location and,
after approximately ten minutes, the officer observed the Cruze leaving the
Methuen Apartments.
[¶6] The officer then followed the Cruze to a nearby store parking lot in
Methuen, where the vehicle parked for approximately twenty minutes. During
this time, the occupants of the Cruze neither entered the store nor interacted
with anyone else in the parking lot. The only movement the officer observed

was the occupants exiting the vehicle and switching seats, so that the male

[¶7] The officer then followed the Cruze as it drove back to Seabrook.
The officer observed no erratic driving or traffic violations, although she
testified that she noticed “excessive blinker usage” by the Cruze. The officer
called other officers on patrol in Seabrook and requested that they pull the
Cruze over for reasonable suspicion of drug possession. The officer then
participated in the traffic stop after the other officers pulled over the Cruze.
Police identified the male driver as the defendant, and the female passenger as
the daughter of the registered owner of the vehicle. The defendant told officers
he had purchased fentanyl, which officers located on the female passenger’s
person. Officers also determined that the defendant was a certified habitual
offender and subsequently arrested him. The defendant was later charged with
one count of operating after certification as a habitual offender.
[¶8] The defendant moved to suppress the evidence obtained as a result
of the traffic stop, asserting that police lacked reasonable suspicion to justify
the stop, and the court held a hearing. At the hearing, the State argued that,
under the totality of the circumstances, the police had reasonable suspicion to
stop the vehicle. The court determined that the previous reports alleging drug
activity, combined with the officer’s observations on the day of the stop, did not
establish reasonable suspicion that the occupants of the Cruze had committed
a drug offense. The court thus concluded that the stop violated the defendant’s
state constitutional rights and accordingly granted the defendant’s motion.
This appeal followed.
[¶9] When reviewing a trial court’s ruling on a motion to suppress, we
accept the court’s factual findings unless they lack support in the record or are
clearly erroneous, and we review its legal conclusions de novo. State v.
Rodriguez, 177 N.H. 599, 603 (2025), 2025 N.H. 43, ¶12. Whether there
existed reasonable suspicion to justify an investigatory stop is a determination
which we review de novo. See State v. Richter, 145 N.H. 640, 641 (2000).
[¶10] Part I, Article 19 of the New Hampshire Constitution protects all
people, their papers, their possessions and their homes from unreasonable
searches and seizures. Rodriguez, 177 N.H. at 603, 2025 N.H. 43, ¶13; see
N.H. CONST. pt. I, art. 19. Warrantless seizures are per se unreasonable
under Part I, Article 19 unless the State proves by a preponderance of the
evidence that the seizure falls within the narrow confines of a judicially crafted
exception. State v. Perez, 173 N.H. 251, 257 (2020).
[¶11] An investigatory stop based on reasonable suspicion is such an
exception. State v. Donovan, 175 N.H. 356, 360 (2022). “In order for a police
officer to undertake an investigatory stop, the officer must have a reasonable
suspicion, based upon specific, articulable facts taken together with rational
3
inferences from those facts, that the particular person stopped has been, is, or
is about to be, engaged in criminal activity.” Id. (quotation omitted); see Terry
v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 20-21 (1968). To determine the sufficiency of an officer’s
suspicion, we consider the articulable facts in light of all surrounding
circumstances, keeping in mind that a trained officer may make inferences and
draw conclusions from conduct that may seem unremarkable to an untrained
observer. Perez, 173 N.H. at 259.
[¶12] Although we recognize that experienced officers’ perceptions are
entitled to deference, this deference should not be blind. Id. A reasonable
suspicion must be more than a hunch, and the suspicion must have a
“particularized and objective basis.” Id. “The articulable facts must lead to
something specific and not simply a general sense that this is probably a bad
person who may have committed some kind of crime.” Id. However,
reasonable suspicion is a less demanding standard than probable cause, and
may be established with information that is less reliable, and different in
quantity or content, than that required to show probable cause. See id. at 260.
[¶13] The State argues that the trial court erred when it concluded that
the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to justify stopping the Cruze.
Specifically, it contends that the court erred when it determined that it was
possible that the officer observed only innocent activity, thereby discounting
the significance of the officer’s observations. It also contends that the court did
not consider the totality of the circumstances and instead viewed each of the
events in isolation. The defendant counters that the court correctly determined
that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to justify the stop. We conclude
that, considering the totality of the circumstances, the facts found by the trial
court establish that the officer had reasonable suspicion to justify the stop.
[¶14] The officer’s observations on the day of the stop — viewed in the
context of the ongoing drug investigations — linked the Cruze and its
occupants to suspected drug activity. Specifically, the officer observed a
vehicle, registered to a female resident of the Seabrook Apartments and
suspected to be involved in drug sales in Seabrook, travel from Seabrook to the
Methuen Apartments — a location which the officer knew to be connected with
previous sales of drugs to confidential informants in Seabrook. The officer was
also aware of separate reports that the Cruze’s registered owner was involved in
drug activity at the Seabrook Apartments. She observed that a woman was the
driver of the vehicle, and reasonably believed that the female driver was the
owner of the vehicle. See Richter, 145 N.H. at 641-42 (explaining it was
reasonable for officer to infer driver was owner of vehicle where officer
“observed nothing that would indicate that the driver was not the owner”). The
officer then observed the vehicle stay at a location, known to be connected with
previous sales of drugs, for a short period of time which, based on her training
and experience, appeared consistent with a drug transaction. An officer
trained in drug investigations could reasonably infer that a short visit to a
4
location connected with drug sales may be associated with a drug transaction.
See State v. Vadnais, 141 N.H. 68, 70-71 (1996).
[¶15] Thereafter, the officer observed the vehicle’s occupants park for
approximately twenty minutes in a store parking lot without entering the store.
As described in the trial court’s order, the officer found the twenty-minute stop
to be “abnormal” and “suspicious,” and she “suspected that the [Cruze’s]
occupants were either concealing or using narcotics while in the . . . parking
lot.” Contrary to the trial court’s view, we conclude, based on the officer’s
training, experience, and previous observations, that she could reasonably
infer under the circumstances that the occupants’ behavior was consistent
with drug activity. See Perez, 173 N.H. at 259; see also State v. Roach, 141
N.H. 64, 65-66 (1996) (describing how area known for drug activity and early
morning hour provided context for defendant’s “furtive behavior” and “cast a
more suspicious light on his own actions”); State v. Hood, 141 N.H. 196, 198-
99 (1996) (noting that police observed “suspicious and incriminating behavior”
when defendant entered a bar, exited with another individual, and took a short
drive before returning to the bar, and concluding that “[i]n light of the
surrounding circumstances, those actions were consistent with a drug
purchase”). We further conclude that, taken together, the above facts amount
to reasonable suspicion that the Cruze’s occupants were engaged in drug
activity.
[¶16] We are not persuaded by the defendant’s arguments to the
contrary. First, the defendant argues that the tips alleging that the Cruze and
the Methuen Apartments were involved in drug activity were not reliable. The
State contends this argument is not preserved. We need not decide whether
the issue is preserved because we conclude that the tips were sufficiently
reliable to contribute to the officer’s reasonable suspicion to stop the Cruze.
The trial court noted in its order that it “[did] not discount the validity of these
tips,” but ultimately concluded that they were “too tenuously connected to the
observations of otherwise exclusively innocent behavior to amount to
reasonable suspicion in this case.” We interpret this language as finding that
the tips provided to police concerning drug activity involving the Cruze and the
Methuen Apartments were reliable. See State v. Surrell, 171 N.H. 82, 88
(2018) (“The interpretation of a trial court order presents a question of law for
us to decide.”). To the extent the defendant challenges this finding, we
conclude that it is supported by the record. See Rodriguez, 177 N.H. at 603,
2025 N.H. 43, ¶12 (stating that, when reviewing a trial court’s ruling on a
motion to suppress, we will uphold the court’s factual findings unless they lack
record support or are clearly erroneous).
[¶17] As to the Cruze and its owner, the record supports that the police
received information from individuals — including confidential informants —
naming the owner as involved in drug activity at the Seabrook Apartments.
This information was consistent with the separate tip identifying the Cruze and
5
alleging its involvement in drug activity at the Seabrook Apartments, given that
police were able to determine from the Cruze’s registration both the owner’s
name and her address at the Seabrook Apartments. See State v. Melanson,
140 N.H. 199, 201-02 (1995) (noting that police “were able to corroborate
certain details” provided in anonymous tip, including vehicle’s make, model,
color, and license plate number, and that “this corroboration can be used to
establish credibility and reliability”); cf. State v. Christy, 138 N.H. 352, 353-54,
359-60 (1994) (noting in probable cause analysis that, in addition to primary
tip, police had “received several other tips,” some of which were anonymous,
within narrow timeframe that alleged defendant was involved in drug activity).
[¶18] As to the Methuen Apartments, the officer became aware of drug
activity at that location through controlled purchases of drugs by confidential
informants, not anonymous tips. See Melanson, 140 N.H. at 201 (“An
informant who has personally observed incriminating behavior has a stronger
basis of knowledge than does an informant who relates not what he knows
personally, but what he has heard others say.” (quotation omitted)); see also
State v. Hazen, 131 N.H. 196, 198, 202 (1988) (concluding that controlled
purchase of drugs from defendant’s residence “substantiated the informant’s
allegations” that he had purchased drugs from defendant). We therefore
disagree with the defendant that the tips were insufficiently reliable to
contribute to the officer’s reasonable suspicion that the Cruze’s occupants were
engaged in drug activity.
[¶19] The defendant further contends that there were innocent
explanations for his activities and, absent concrete reasons for concluding
otherwise, the officer’s observations of potentially innocent activity could not
amount to reasonable suspicion. See State v. Blesdell-Moore, 166 N.H. 183,
189 (2014) (“We think it impossible for a combination of wholly innocent
factors to combine into a suspicious conglomeration unless there are concrete
reasons for such an interpretation.” (quotation and brackets omitted)). We
disagree that the potentially innocent explanations for the defendant’s activities
undermined reasonable suspicion in this case.
[¶20] Reasonable suspicion “may be based upon activity that is
consistent with both guilty and innocent behavior.” State v. McKinnon-
Andrews, 151 N.H. 19, 26-27 (2004). “Although any one factor in the totality of
circumstances may seem innocent or innocuous in isolation, we consider the
[officer’s] observations together and in light of the reasonable inferences that an
officer who is experienced in detecting and investigating drug trafficking may
draw.” Perez, 173 N.H. at 262. As described above, even though the
defendant’s activities may have appeared innocent when viewed in isolation,
the context of the ongoing investigations into drug sales at both the Seabrook
and the Methuen Apartments cast a more suspicious light on the defendant’s
activities and the officer could draw reasonable inferences from that conduct
based on her training and experience. See Roach, 141 N.H. at 65-66; State v.
6
Pellicci, 133 N.H. 523, 530 (1990) (noting that, although some of defendant’s
activities may have appeared innocent in isolation, officers who were
experienced in investigating drug transactions could reasonably infer under
circumstances that defendant made multiple brief trips from club to parking lot
in order to sell drugs).
[¶21] Based on the totality of the circumstances, we conclude that the
facts known to the officer, combined with her observations of the activities of
the Cruze and its occupants on the day of the stop, were sufficient to establish
reasonable suspicion that the occupants of the Cruze were engaged in illegal
drug activity. In reaching the opposite conclusion, the trial court failed to
adequately consider the totality of the circumstances, and in doing so, it
effectively imposed a higher burden on the State than necessary to establish
reasonable suspicion. See Perez, 173 N.H. at 260; United States v. Sokolow,
490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989) (explaining that suspicion necessary for investigative
detention “is considerably less than proof of wrongdoing by a preponderance of
the evidence”).
[¶22] We have considered the defendant’s remaining arguments and have
concluded that they do not warrant further discussion. See Vogel v. Vogel, 137
N.H. 321, 322 (1993). We therefore reverse the trial court’s order granting the
defendant’s motion to suppress and remand for further proceedings.
Reversed and remanded.
