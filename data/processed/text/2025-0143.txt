THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE
SUPREME COURT
In Case No. 2025-0143, In the Matter of Scott Billodeau and
Shannon Billodeau, the court on February 5, 2026, issued the
following order:
The petitioner’s motion to strike the respondent’s reply brief and
documents submitted therewith is granted in part and denied in part. To the
extent the petitioner seeks to strike the documents labeled, “additional
information,” that the respondent submitted with her reply brief, the motion is
granted on the basis that the documents constitute an untimely-filed appendix
for purposes of Rule 13(3). Otherwise, the motion is denied.
The court has reviewed the written arguments and the record submitted
on appeal, and has determined to resolve the case by way of this order. See
Sup. Ct. R. 20(3). The respondent, Shannon Billodeau, appeals a final decree
entered by the Circuit Court (Tessier, J.), following a two-day final hearing, in
her divorce from the petitioner, Scott Billodeau. On appeal, the respondent
challenges the trial court’s determination that a postnuptial agreement is
unenforceable on grounds that it was procured by concealment of a material
fact and that the circumstances had so changed since its execution as to
render it unconscionable. The respondent raises numerous additional
arguments challenging the trial court’s property distribution, its awards of
alimony and child support to the respondent, its parenting plan, and its
decision not to grant the respondent a “marital restraining order.” Finally, the
respondent argues that the trial court was improperly biased. We affirm.
“Unlike parties to a commercial contract, . . . spouses are traditionally
regarded as fiduciaries of one another.” Estate of Wilber, 165 N.H. 246, 251
(2013). Thus, although a postnuptial agreement “carries with it a presumption
of validity,” it is scrutinized more closely than an ordinary commercial contract.
Id. at 252 (quotation omitted). A party seeking to invalidate a postnuptial
agreement must prove either that (1) it “was obtained through fraud, duress or
mistake, or through misrepresentation or nondisclosure of a material fact”; (2)
it is unconscionable; or (3) “the facts and circumstances have so changed since
the agreement was executed as to make the agreement unenforceable.” Id. at
252 (quotations omitted). We defer to the trial court’s findings as to the
enforceability of a postnuptial agreement unless they are so plainly erroneous
that they could not be reasonably made, and we will uphold its decision unless
it is unsupported by the evidence or plainly erroneous as a matter of law. See
In the Matter of Nizhnikov & Nizhnikov, 168 N.H. 525, 528 (2016); Estate of
Wilber, 165 N.H. at 251.
The trial court has broad discretion in fashioning a final divorce decree.
In the Matter of Spenard & Spenard, 167 N.H. 1, 3 (2014). Its discretion
includes decisions concerning alimony, child support, and property
distribution. Id. Its discretion likewise includes decisions concerning whether
to restrain a party to a divorce from abusing, contacting, or harassing the other
party. See RSA 458:16, I(a)-(d) (2018); In the Matter of Peirano & Larsen 155
N.H. 738, 751-52 (2007) (applying unsustainable exercise of discretion
standard to decision to continue a restraining order under RSA 458:16, I).
Finally, the trial court’s discretion includes decisions regarding the parties’
parenting rights and responsibilities relative to their children. See In the
Matter of Kurowski & Kurowski, 161 N.H. 578, 585 (2011). We will not
overturn the trial court’s rulings on such matters absent an unsustainable
exercise of discretion, reviewing the record only to determine whether it
contains an objective basis to sustain the court’s discretionary judgments.
Spenard, 167 N.H. at 3. If the trial court reasonably could have reached its
findings of fact on the evidence before it, they will stand. Id. We defer to the
trial court’s judgment in evaluating the credibility of witnesses and determining
the weight of the evidence presented, recognizing that the trial court is free to
accept or reject, in whole or in part, the testimony of any witness or party, and
that it is not required to believe even uncontested evidence. In the Matter of
Aube & Aube, 158 N.H. 458, 465 (2009).
It is the burden of the appealing party, in this case the respondent, to
demonstrate reversible error, and in so doing, to support the issues raised on
appeal with fully developed legal argument. Gallo v. Traina, 166 N.H. 737, 740
(2014); State v. Blackmer, 149 N.H. 47, 49 (2003). These procedural
requirements are not relaxed for self-represented parties. See In the Matter of
Birmingham & Birmingham, 154 N.H. 51, 56-57 (2006). Based upon our
review of the trial court’s orders, including its well-reasoned narrative order,
the respondent’s arguments, the relevant law, and the record submitted on
appeal, we conclude that the respondent has not demonstrated that the trial
court’s decision not to enforce the postnuptial agreement was unsupported by
the evidence or plainly erroneous as a matter of law, or that the court
unsustainably exercised its discretion with respect to any of the remaining
decisions that the respondent challenges. See Gallo, 166 N.H. at 740;
Nizhnikov, 168 N.H. at 528; Spenard, 167 N.H. at 3. To the extent the
respondent argues that the trial court was improperly biased, based upon our
review of the record, the respondent has not established that a reasonable
2
person would have questioned the impartiality of the trial court. See Sup. Ct.
R. 38, Canon 2.11; State v. Bader, 148 N.H. 265, 268 (2002).
Affirmed.
MacDonald, C.J., and Donovan, Countway, and Gould, JJ., concurred.
